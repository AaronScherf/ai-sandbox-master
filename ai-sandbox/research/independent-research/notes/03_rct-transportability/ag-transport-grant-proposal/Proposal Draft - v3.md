# How far can environmental data carry crop-response predictions into conflict-affected regions? A held-out test and a trial design for Sudan and South Sudan

## Project summary
**Overview.** Decisions about fertilizer, planting and food assistance in Sudan and South Sudan depend on how much grain a field will yield and how much more it will yield with a nutrient added. Most of that land has never been measured, because fighting prevents field trials and surveys. Predictions must therefore be carried in from places that were measured. We ask a question that natural science can answer: **do soil, climate and vegetation data predict the yield and the fertilizer response of fields where no one has measured them, and how large and how uncertain is the error?** We will test this first on existing data, by withholding Somalia, a conflict-affected country with enough yield records, and the most recent years, and then in new fertilizer-response trials in Sudan and South Sudan whose sites are chosen to fill the gaps in environmental coverage.

**Intellectual merit.** Transfer of crop response across environments is the central unresolved problem in using sparse agronomic data. The best established method, the OFRA Inference Tool, selects response functions from sites with similar soil and climate, but its published validation is in-sample, and it has never been tested on held-out sites or conflict-affected land. A pilot with open data finds that, with proxy inputs, environmental similarity improves prediction of a held-out site by only about 4%, and by nothing across countries. We will determine whether richer environmental data, a hierarchical model, or new sites can do better, and what level of accuracy is possible at all.

**Broader impacts.** An honest accuracy bound tells planners where a prediction is usable and where only a trial will do. Open data, code and a replicable pipeline support work in other regions where measurement is unsafe.

## 1. The problem
Maize and sorghum yields in sub-Saharan Africa are low and variable: the coefficient of variation is often above 40% and mean yield is 10-30% of the rainfed potential (Wortmann et al. 2017). Whether a fertilizer pays depends on the response curve at a field, which varies with soil, rainfall, temperature and previous management (Nziguheba et al. 2021; Kihara et al. 2016; Rurinda et al. 2020). Trials are expensive and have clustered where access is easy. Whether a trial result could be used depends on whether fertilizer can be bought. Smallholders in South Sudan do not use chemical fertilizer on field crops (FAO/WFP 2025); in Sudan fertilizer is imported (over half from the Gulf) and its price rose from about $11 to about $50 per 50 kg bag of urea between May 2025 and May 2026 in one irrigated scheme (Reuters/AP, May 2026; a single farmer's account, irrigated land). The farming base is large but only partly addressable. South Sudan has about 1.34 million farming households on 1.26 million hectares of cereals, area up 7% in 2025 (FAO/WFP 2026); mapped cropland there is near zero only because plots are small and scattered. Sudan has about 15 million hectares of cereals and its 2025 output fell 22%. About 40% of South Sudan's farming households and 35% of Sudan's mapped cropland lie in cells with low recent conflict intensity (at most 10 weighted violent events a year within 50 km), and in Sudan the low-risk belt (Gedaref, Kassala) is largely mechanized. A phone survey of 2,101 Sudanese farming households found over three-quarters used fertilizer in the 2023/24 winter season, but just over half planned to plant nothing in the 2024 summer season (IFPRI 2026; Darfur not covered; sorghum not among the crops listed for fertilizer use). In Sudan and South Sudan, trials are rare: we found no plot-level fertilizer trial in South Sudan, and the best recent yield assessment for Sudan calibrates satellite vegetation index to crop cuts in four states with no held-out test (CIMMYT 2025, 2024/25 season).

## 2. What is known and the gap
- **Response information exists, but is concentrated.** The OFRA database holds about 5,500 response functions from 16 countries (Wortmann et al. 2017). The pilot's version (August 2023) has 6,146 function rows at 928 sites. None are in Somalia, Sudan or South Sudan, and of 517 trial site-years since 2010 only 17 had 50 or more violent events within 50 km (15 of them in Rwanda).
- **The accepted transfer method is validated only in-sample.** The OFRA Inference Tool queries seven environmental layers (aridity, temperature seasonality, elevation, distance from the equator, soil pH, sand, organic carbon). The paper compares pooled pseudo-R2 across domain definitions; it does not predict held-out sites (Wortmann et al. 2017; the companion extrapolation study is Tesfahunegn and Wortmann 2017).
- **Spatial validation matters and is contested.** Standard cross-validation overstates skill when data are spatially clustered (Ploton et al. 2020); others argue that the right test depends on the use (Wadoux et al. 2021). We therefore specify the validation target in advance (Section 4).
- **Evidence in the target region is thin.** For South Sudan sorghum, a survey of about 2,100 farmers found that adding conflict exposure to environmental data added little (Karongo et al. 2025); that data is available only on request. Satellite yield models in Africa reach R2 of about 0.5 against farm data (Burke and Lobell 2017).
- **Effect transfer is hard in general.** Treatment effects generalize poorly across settings (Vivalt 2020), and hierarchical Bayesian models are the standard way to say how far (Meager 2019).

**Gap.** No study reports, for a conflict-affected country, how accurately environmental data predict held-out yield and fertilizer response, or whether a few well-placed new trials would change that.

## 3. Preliminary results (open data; first-pass; not independently reproduced)
We assembled HarvestStat Africa subnational yields (Lee et al. 2025) (828 administrative units, 2010-2023), ACLED violent events, CHIRPS rainfall, MODIS NDVI, static climate and soil layers, and the OFRA database.
1. **Somalia can serve as a holdout.** It has 190 exposed and 322 non-exposed unit-years for sorghum and 226 and 337 for maize (exposed means 50 or more violent events in the crop-season years); Nigeria passes for maize only. Sudan sorghum has 65 and 188. South Sudan has no usable sorghum or maize yield after 2012.
2. **Weather data carry some signal within a unit, little across countries.** Within-unit R2 for yield anomalies is 0.13-0.19 for rainfall and 0.07-0.21 for NDVI in Somalia and Sudan; a slope fitted on other countries has out-of-country R2 of 0.02-0.07 and is strongly negative in Niger (-0.4 to -1.0).
3. **The Inference Tool adds little in held-out tests.** For maize N (271 sites, proxy layers), choosing similar sites improves the predicted response curve over a pooled curve by 4% when the site is withheld and loses 2% when its country is withheld. Our replication of the paper's own table is partial (pooled response correlates 0.56-0.59 across nine sites; pseudo-R2 not reproduced), so this is a lower-confidence baseline.
4. **Trials are only modestly better than their surroundings.** Where a trial and a HarvestStat yield share a unit, crop and year (94 unit-years), the trial's unfertilised yield is a median 1.4 times (maize) and 1.3 times (sorghum) the unit yield, with an interquartile range reaching 1.0. Area yield statistics could help calibrate site-specific response, but the overlap is small and mostly outside the Horn.

**What the pilot does not show.** It does not test fertilizer response in any conflict-affected country, because none exists in the data. That absence is the justification for the trials below.

## 4. Research plan
**Hypotheses (falsifiable).**
- **H1, sufficiency:** environmental covariates alone do not predict the held-out country's yield and fertilizer response well enough to decide whether fertilizer pays. The criterion is fixed before testing: the 80% prediction interval for the response at 50 kg N/ha must exclude zero and exclude break-even (50 kg N x value-cost ratio 2 x price of N / price of grain; 120 kg grain/ha at pre-2026 urea prices and 540 kg/ha at 2026 prices, with an assumed grain price), for at least half of held-out units. We will report the share and the interval width, whichever way it falls.
- **H2, distance:** prediction error rises with distance from the nearest training sites in covariate space; once environment is accounted for, conflict exposure adds little (as in Karongo et al. 2025).
- **H3, design:** sites chosen to cover empty parts of covariate space lower transfer error more than the same number of convenient sites.
- **H4, area information:** area-level yield statistics improve prediction of site-specific response beyond environmental covariates alone.

**Aim 1: retrospective holdout (no new data collection; months 1-9).** Train on HarvestStat and OFRA from every country except Somalia; predict Somalia. Second stage: train on all years before 2020 and predict later years. Compare (a) the Inference Tool as published, (b) a hierarchical Bayesian model with unit and country effects (Meager 2019), and (c) the same with satellite NDVI (cropland-masked) and area yield statistics. Validation target: country-level prediction accuracy for unseen units, so we use leave-one-country-out and report spatial cross-validation alongside it (Ploton et al. 2020; Wadoux et al. 2021). Outcome: prediction error and interval coverage by distance in covariate space, with and without conflict exposure.

**Aim 2: proxy assessment (months 6-12).** Test whether conflict-affected regions with measurements (north-east Nigeria, the Sahel) predict Somalia and Sudan better than their environmental distance alone implies. The pilot finds that Somalia's nearest climate neighbours are only slightly farther from it than its own units are from each other (0.19 against 0.14 on standardized rainfall mean and variability) and are rarely conflict-exposed (3%).

**Aim 3: prospective trials (months 12-42; the part that needs funding; released only if the Aim 1 gate below is met).** Replicated fertilizer-response trials with crop cuts (nitrogen first, five rates; phosphorus if funds allow) on sorghum, and maize where grown, at about 30 sites per country in Sudan and South Sudan, with national partners. Site selection: choose sites that maximize coverage of the environmental covariate space not represented in the existing data (H3), among cells whose ACLED conflict-intensity score (violent events within 50 km over the last 24 months, weighted by fatalities) is below a cap that is set with the field partners (our model suggests about 10 weighted events a year, which keeps about 45% of cells and loses little information), with a random subset of convenient sites as the comparison. In South Sudan the arm is conditional on a credible fertilizer supply or subsidy route. Outcome: whether Aim 1 predictions, made before the trials and registered, hold; and how much the interval narrows when the new sites are added.

**Decision gates (from the pilot's trial-size model).** (1) *Aim 1 gate:* if held-out covariate prediction beats a pooled curve by little (the pilot finds 2-4%), each new trial informs essentially its own location and covering a country needs hundreds of trials; the grant then funds a smaller, price-conditional design, or a data-collection and supply study instead. If covariates transfer well (the model's optimistic bound), about 30 trials per country achieve most of the gain (posterior SD of the area response falls from about 290 to about 160 kg/ha and 42-47% of cells (30 trials) to 50-59% (80 trials) become decidable at 2026 prices). (2) *Price gate:* if the break-even exceeds what the Aim 1 model predicts for the typical unit (at 2026 prices the mean OFRA sorghum response, 476 kg/ha, is below the 540 kg/ha break-even), trials are targeted at the subset of sites predicted to respond, not spread across the covariate space. (3) *Access gate:* the conflict cap changes which sites are eligible far more than how much is learned. (4) *Use gate:* before any trial is funded, two or three named decision-makers (for example input-supply programs and seed/fertilizer distribution agencies) state what response would change what they buy or distribute; if none will, the trials do not proceed.

**Risks and fallbacks.** Access may close sites; we oversample and use satellite-verified plot locations. If prediction proves good (H1 rejected), the result is equally useful: it says where trials can be skipped. Yield records inside insecure units may be missing for non-random reasons; we test reporting rates against exposure (no signal in the pilot) and report bounds.

## 5. Why this needs a grant
Open data cannot answer the question: there is no fertilizer-response record in the target countries, and subnational yield data there are too coarse (Sudan states average about 57,000 km2, Somali districts 5,700). Collecting trials in conflict-affected areas costs more than the same trials elsewhere; at an assumed $20,000-60,000 per site over three seasons, 30 sites per country cost $0.6-1.8 million (unit costs are placeholders: a search found no published per-site cost for fertilizer-response trials in Africa, or any conflict premium, so the budget is a parameter to be set from partner quotes). Collecting trials requires field teams, seed and fertilizer, crop-cut equipment, and local partners. The retrospective Aims 1-2 are low cost and de-risk the expensive Aim 3.

## 6. Feasibility, team and timeline
All code, data sources and checksums for the pilot are in an open repository, and Aims 1-2 reuse it. Aim 3 depends on partner organizations in Sudan and South Sudan; we do not yet have commitments, and the USAID-funded CIMMYT programme that produced the 2024/25 assessment may not continue (status unconfirmed). Timeline: 42 months; costs not yet estimated.

## 7. Broader impacts
Predictions that carry an honest error bound help agencies decide where a trial is needed and where not. The tools (open pipeline, pre-registered validation, covariate-coverage site design) apply to other regions where measurement is unsafe. Training of national agronomists in crop-cut and trial design is part of Aim 3.
