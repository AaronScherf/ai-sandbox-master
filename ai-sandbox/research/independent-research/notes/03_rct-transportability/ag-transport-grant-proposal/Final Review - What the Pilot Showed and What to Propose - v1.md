# Final review: what the exploratory pilot showed, and what a proposal can honestly claim (v1)

*2026-11-17. A course-project wrap-up of the whole process. The proposal is hypothetical: no organization has been contacted. Supersedes nothing; it consolidates [Reference Log v4](Reference%20Log%20-%20Four%20Searches%20-%20v4.md), [Addenda v1](Feasibility%20Addendum%20-%20Fertilizer%20Access%20and%20Trial%20Size%20-%20v1.md), [v2](Feasibility%20Addendum%20-%20Trial%20Costs%20and%20Farming%20Base%20-%20v2.md) and [v3](Feasibility%20Addendum%20-%20Decision-Makers%20for%20the%20Use%20Gate%20-%20v3.md), and [Proposal Draft v4](Proposal%20Draft%20-%20v4.md). Code and tables: [`projects/ag-transport-pilot/`](../../projects/ag-transport-pilot/README.md). Every result is run on acquired data by one analyst and is not independently reproduced.*

## 1. Bottom line

1. **The question is real and under-tested.** The accepted method for carrying crop-response information to unmeasured places (the OFRA Inference Tool, Wortmann et al. 2017) is validated only in-sample. Nobody has reported how well environmental data predict fertilizer response in a conflict-affected country.
2. **On open data the answer so far is "barely".** With proxy inputs, selecting environmentally similar sites beats a pooled average by 4% for a held-out site and loses 2% for a held-out country. Rainfall and satellite vegetation (NDVI) explain 7-21% of year-to-year yield variation inside an area but 2-7% across countries.
3. **The target countries are not data-poor by accident.** OFRA has no site in Somalia, Sudan or South Sudan; South Sudan has no usable yield series after 2012; of 517 OFRA trial site-years since 2010 only 17 had 50 or more violent events within 50 km.
4. **Whether fertilizer pays is mostly a price question.** Break-even is 120 kg grain/ha at 2025 urea prices and 540 kg/ha at May 2026 prices; the OFRA mean sorghum response is 476 kg/ha. South Sudan smallholders use no fertilizer; Sudan imports collapsed from about 450,000 to about 50,000 tonnes in 2024.
5. **There are enough farmers, but the addressable base is smaller.** About 1.34 million farming households in South Sudan and about 15 million hectares of cereals in Sudan; roughly 40% of South Sudan's households and 35% of Sudan's mapped cropland sit in cells with low recent conflict intensity.
6. **Decision-makers exist and are short of money.** Input programs (Sustain Sudan, FAO, the Agricultural Bank, World Bank/FAO RALP) plan subsidies and distribution but are under-funded, so the research can be framed as **improving the targeting efficiency of scarce inputs**, provided prediction can actually rank sites. That proviso is the hypothesis, not a premise (section 7).
7. **A grant should be staged.** Fund the cheap hold-out analysis first; release trial money only if covariates transfer or prices make trials decisive; require a named user before fielding trials.

## 2. How the design evolved
Started as a conservation and conflict study; dropped (no conflict-zone literature, weak link to humanitarian response). Chosen "Design B": predictability first. Hold-out prediction on OFRA and HarvestStat Africa, Somalia as a country holdout plus a temporal holdout, then a grant for new trials in Sudan and South Sudan at sites chosen for covariate coverage. Policy: keep it natural science, not development economics, and keep the planner-in-the-loop work for the thesis chapters. Since then, three things reshaped it: the hold-out results (section 4), the price and access evidence (section 5) and the user evidence (section 7).

## 3. What the data are and where they run out
| Source | What I used | Limit that matters |
|---|---|---|
| HarvestStat Africa v1.2 (Lee et al. 2025) | 828 administrative units, 12,899 unit-crop-years, 2010-2023 | Units are large: Somali districts about 5,700 km2, Sudan states about 57,000 km2 |
| ACLED (violent events) | Exposure = 50 or more events in the crop-season years; weighted risk scores for cells | Reporting, not true risk; South Sudan exports end October 2025; not committed to the repo |
| CHIRPS v2.0 | Monthly rainfall by unit | Single-season window bug fixed (section 9) |
| MODIS MOD13Q1 NDVI via Earth Engine | 250 m, monthly maximum, 139,104 unit-months | No crop mask |
| Static layers (Earth Engine proxies) | Aridity (TerraClimate), temperature seasonality (WorldClim), elevation (SRTM), soil pH, sand, organic carbon (iSDAsoil) | Proxies for the tool's own rasters; organic-carbon scaling unchecked |
| OFRA response database (Aug 2023) | 6,146 function rows at 928 sites | None in the Horn; 17 of 517 trial site-years exposed |

Somalia is the only target with enough exposed and non-exposed yield unit-years: sorghum 190 and 322, maize 226 and 337; Sudan sorghum 65 and 188; South Sudan none after 2012. Where an OFRA trial and a HarvestStat yield share a unit, crop and year (94 unit-years), the trial's unfertilized yield is a median 1.43 times (maize) and 1.26 times (sorghum) the unit yield, interquartile range reaching 1.0: modestly better land, not the large selection effect I first reported.

## 4. Prediction results
- **Weather inside an area.** Within-unit R2 for yield anomalies: Somalia sorghum 0.19 (rain), 0.15 (NDVI), 0.20 (both); Sudan sorghum 0.15, 0.21, 0.24; Somalia maize 0.13, 0.07, 0.13.
- **Weather across countries.** Out-of-country R2 about 0.02-0.07; median over 21 country-crop sets 0.03 (rain), 0.02 (NDVI), 0.04 (both); strongly negative in Niger (-0.4 to -1.0). NDVI is worth keeping as a covariate and is not a breakthrough. This is inspired by, not a replication of, the CIMMYT 2024/25 Sudan sorghum assessment.
- **OFRA Inference Tool, replicated.** Partial: pooled response correlates 0.56-0.59 with the paper's across nine sites; pseudo-R2 not reproduced (0.01-0.06 against 0.19-0.53). The cause is unseparated (proxy rasters, function filter or R2 definition); treat held-out numbers as lower-confidence.
- **New held-out test (not in the paper).** Skill versus the pooled curve: **+0.04 leave-site-out, -0.02 leave-country-out**; exposed and non-exposed sites (15 and 81) cannot be told apart.
- **Gaussian-process version.** Leave-country-out skill 0.018; fitted lengthscale 0.16 standardized units against a median distance of 1.4 (sorghum) from Sudan's cells to the nearest OFRA site (0.36 between OFRA sites). Covariates carry almost no information across that gap, and forcing longer reach makes the covariate-explained variance collapse, so long-range transfer is unsupported, not disproved.

## 5. Fertilizer access, price and break-even
- **South Sudan.** Smallholders on field crops use no chemical fertilizer, pesticide or herbicide (FAO/WFP CFSAM 2025, read); small amounts on vegetables near Uganda; about $1.79 million of nitrogen fertilizer imported in 2023 (summary). No price series found.
- **Sudan.** Over half of fertilizer imports come from the Gulf; imports reportedly fell from about 450,000 to about 50,000 tonnes in 2024; a Gezira farmer reports urea at about $50 a bag in May 2026 against $11 a year earlier; surveys show fertilizer +67% year on year; world urea rose from $392.50 a tonne (December 2025) to $725.63 (March 2026). One farmer, irrigated land. IFPRI Working Paper 27 (read) has no prices; it finds over three-quarters of 2,101 surveyed households used fertilizer in the 2023/24 winter season (sorghum not among crops listed) and just over half planned to plant nothing in summer 2024.
- **Break-even.** BE = 50 kg N x value-cost ratio 2 x (price of N / price of grain). Urea is 46% N. With an assumed grain price of $0.40/kg (no sourced price found): 120 kg grain/ha at $11 a bag, 540 kg/ha at $50. Mercy Corps (May 2026, read) says rainfed sorghum and millet are significantly less fertilizer-dependent in Sudan, though some semi-mechanized rainfed sorghum uses chemical fertilizer.

## 6. Trial-size and risk model (Addendum v1; scripts 14, 15)
Response at 50 kg N/ha modelled as a Gaussian process over seven covariates, calibrated on OFRA site means; 0.25-degree cells (553 in Sudan, 843 in South Sudan with aridity index at least 0.2); risk = violent ACLED events within 50 km, last 24 months, weighted 1 + ln(1 + fatalities), per year; greedy site choice under a risk cap; Monte Carlo of "decidable" cells (80% interval excludes break-even).
- **Data-fitted model:** 80 trials per country lower the area-level SD only from 429 to about 380 kg/ha; 16-28% of cells become decidable at pre-spike prices and 6-9% at spike prices. Covering a country at this resolution takes hundreds of trials.
- **Imposed optimistic bound** (covariates explain half of between-site variance, lengthscale 1.5): about 30 trials per country capture most of the gain; SD about 290 to about 160; 42-47% of Sudan and South Sudan cells decidable at 2026 prices after 30 trials, 50-59% after 80.
- **Risk cap acts through eligibility, not information.** Cells with weighted risk at most 2, 10 and 40 are 19%, 45% and 80% of Sudan's domain (23%, 44%, 74% of South Sudan's); information barely differs across caps. A cap near 10 loses little and excludes the worst areas.
- **Cost.** No published per-site trial cost found for Africa or conflict settings (only $5.73 per breeding-trial row plus $1 per 130 km). My $20,000-60,000 per site is an unvalidated placeholder; 30 sites per country would be $0.6-1.8 million on it.

## 7. The farming base and the users
- **Farming base** (Addendum v2). South Sudan: 1.34 million farming households on 1.26 million ha of cereals (about 0.9 ha each), area up 7% in 2025 as returnees and urban households farm; WorldCover shows near-zero cropland there because plots are small and scattered. Sudan: WorldCover cropland 16.3 million ha agrees with FAO's 15.3 million ha planted to sorghum, millet and wheat in 2024/25. 2025 cereal output was 5.2 million tonnes (-22%; sorghum -25%). Low-risk Gedaref and Kassala hold about 4.8 million ha of cropland but are largely mechanized; Al Jazirah (median risk 380) and Sennar are not low-risk.
- **Users** (Addendum v3, ranked): Sustain Sudan (Sustain Africa, IFDC, CIMMYT; 260,000-tonne, at least 30% discount program; a stated 40% yield-gain assumption; no trials component; funder unnamed); the Ministry of Agriculture and Irrigation and Agricultural Bank (financing 40% of 25 million feddans; Finance Minister sought sorghum and millet input finance on 8 June 2026); FAO Sudan (827,000 households with seed in 2025; 2026 appeals funded at 11% of emergency agriculture needs); the South Sudan FAO/WFP assessment (county yield estimates); South Sudan's Ministry and RALP (World Bank, $62.5 million, 2021-2026); CIMMYT SASAS (status after November 2025 unknown).
- **The targeting-efficiency framing is supported, with a condition.** Programs are short of money and fertilizer is scarce, so *where* it goes matters. Sustain Sudan's stated aims (260,000 tonnes over 5 million ha) imply about 52 kg of product per hectare (my arithmetic; the program's own acreage figures disagree between pages). But targeting only pays if a prediction can rank sites. Back-of-envelope (not run on data): choosing the best 30% of sites by a predictor with correlation rho to the truth raises mean response by rho x sigma x 1.16, where sigma is the between-site SD (551 kg/ha for OFRA sorghum). At the pilot's cross-country skill (R2 about 0.02, rho about 0.13) that is about **85 kg/ha**, below even the cheap-fertilizer break-even of 120; if covariates explained half the variance (rho about 0.71) it is about **450 kg/ha**. Which world we are in is the hold-out test of Aim 1. So the proposal should state targeting value as a hypothesis with a number attached, not as the premise.

## 9. Corrections log (so the paper can be trusted)
1. CHIRPS rainfall used a single-season window; replaced with the union of seasons, and earlier claims (including "3 times farther in climate distance") were withdrawn. v3 is correct, v1-v2 numbers are not.
2. OFRA's CoefA is the **plateau**, not the control (control = a - b): trial control ratios are 1.43 and 1.26, not 1.9 and 1.7.
3. The Inference Tool replication is partial; the paper's pseudo-R2 was not reproduced.
4. The CHIRPS transfer figures in v3 and v4 use different country sets (0.02 against 0.05-0.06).
5. WorldCover misses South Sudan's smallholder plots; I switched the model's domain from cropland to climate.
6. DISES, named in drafts v1-v3, no longer exists.
7. Mixed counts between sources are flagged, not reconciled (RALP households, Sustain Sudan acreage, FAO appeals).

## 10. What a proposal can claim, and the honest weak points
**Can claim.** (a) The first held-out, conflict-aware test of environmental transfer of fertilizer response; (b) a registered prediction for Somalia (country holdout) and later years (temporal holdout), with a criterion fixed in advance; (c) a covariate-coverage trial design that responds to a risk cap, with a model others can rerun; (d) a use gate and a price gate that stop spending when trials cannot change a decision.
**Cannot claim.** That fertilizer pays in Sudan or South Sudan; that covariates can predict response (the pilot says they barely do); that trials are cost-effective (no cost data); that any user will adopt the output.
**Weak points a reviewer will find.** Proxy inputs; no reproduction of the tool's own pseudo-R2; the break-even rests on an assumed grain price and a single price report; the optimistic model case is imposed, not estimated; the use evidence is mostly search summaries; no site is exposed in OFRA, so conflict degradation cannot be estimated from it at all.
