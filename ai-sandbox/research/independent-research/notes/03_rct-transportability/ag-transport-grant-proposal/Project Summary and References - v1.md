## Summary

Whether nitrogen fertilizer raises crop yield, and by how much, depends on rainfall, temperature and soil, and it is measured in replicated field trials. A review of the main open sources, the OFRA nitrogen-response database and the HarvestStat Africa subnational yield compilation, finds substantial trial and yield records across much of Africa but none for Sudan or South Sudan. These sources are not exhaustive, and the reasons for the gap are not assumed here. Where fertilizer is scarce and prices have risen sharply, as in this region since 2025, decisions must rest on predictions carried in from measured sites elsewhere. The most widely used method, the OFRA Inference Tool, selects response functions from sites with similar aridity, temperature seasonality, elevation, latitude, soil pH, sand content and organic carbon. Its published validation measures how well it fits the sites already in the database. The external-validity question is untested: does a response function hold at a new site, and especially in a new country? The first phase of the project is a review of existing data that validates the approach before any field investment. It withholds whole countries (starting with Somalia) and the most recent years, then measures how accurate the predictions are and whether their confidence intervals are honest. The baseline is the OFRA tool and a simple pooled average, and satellite rainfall and vegetation time series are added to test whether they help. Preliminary analysis with open soil and climate layers suggests the baseline is weak. Similarity-based selection improved on the pooled average by only about 4% for a withheld site and by nothing for a withheld country. Fitted models indicate why. Response changes over short environmental distances, while Sudan's conditions lie roughly four times farther from the nearest measured site than measured sites lie from each other. The central scientific question is how prediction error grows with environmental distance, and how much of it the covariates can explain.

The main proposal is the second phase, which depends on grant funding: replicated fertilizer-response trials on sorghum, with maize where grown, in Sudan and South Sudan. Environmental conditions across both countries can already be mapped from satellite and soil data. What is missing is the yield response under those conditions. Trial sites would therefore be chosen where existing data undersample the conditions, so each trial adds the most information, and kept below a cap on recent conflict events nearby for the safety of field teams. Designing both the predictions and the experiments around conflict intensity is also a useful scientific question in its own right. The sites that can be measured safely may differ systematically from those that cannot, so it matters how well predictions from accessible places transfer to inaccessible ones, and how much information trials can gain when part of the environmental space is off limits. The most interesting question is whether better predictions make fertilizer targeting more efficient. When fertilizer is scarce, the gain from directing it to where it pays depends on two things. One is how much response differs between sites; in the existing sorghum data it varies about as much between sites as its average size. The other is how well predictions can rank sites from most to least responsive. If predictions cannot rank sites, a blind allocation does as well, and only local trials can say where fertilizer pays. The project would measure ranking ability on existing data, then test how much regional trials improve it. It would also estimate the cost-benefit tradeoff of fertilizer under the higher global price and the added difficulty and cost of delivery to farmers in conflict zones. This would show how many trials are worth running before additional trials stop changing the decision. This matters because government and donor groups have proposed fertilizer subsidy and distribution programs for Sudan and South Sudan, and reports suggest they are generally short of funds. They need evidence to justify greater funding and to allocate scarce fertilizer where it is most likely to pay.
