# How far can soil, climate and satellite data carry crop nutrient-response predictions into conflict-affected drylands? A held-out test and a cost-aware trial design for Sudan and South Sudan

## Project summary
**Overview.** In the semi-arid belt of Sudan and South Sudan, whether a nitrogen fertilizer raises sorghum yield, and by how much, depends on rainfall, temperature, soil and previous management. That response has been measured at hundreds of sites in other African countries and at none in Somalia, Sudan or South Sudan, because fighting makes field trials unsafe. Predictions must therefore be carried in from measured places. We ask: **how well do soil, climate and satellite-vegetation data predict the nutrient response of places where it has never been measured, how does the error grow with environmental distance and with conflict, and how many new trials, placed where, would shrink it enough to change a fertilizer decision at realistic costs and prices?** We test this first on existing data by withholding a whole country (Somalia) and the most recent years, and then design, as the funded part, a trial network whose sites are chosen to cover unsampled environmental conditions below a conflict-risk cap.

**Intellectual merit.** Transferring crop response across environments is the central unresolved problem in using sparse agronomic data. The best-established method, the OFRA Inference Tool, selects response functions from sites with similar climate and soil but is validated only in-sample. A pilot with open data finds that, with proxy inputs, similarity selection beats a pooled average by about 4% for a held-out site and by nothing for a held-out country. We will determine whether Earth-observation time series, a hierarchical model or well-placed new sites can do better, and what accuracy is attainable at all.

**Broader impacts.** Fertilizer is scarce in Sudan (imports reportedly fell from about 450,000 to about 50,000 tonnes in 2024) and input programs are underfunded, so where the little available goes matters. A quantified accuracy bound tells planners where a prediction is usable and where only a trial will do, and shows whether better targeting is worth more than the trials cost. The pipeline, code and checksummed data are open and reusable wherever measurement is unsafe.

## 1. Context
**Nutrient response as a natural-science problem.** Cereal response to nitrogen is well described by a saturating curve, yield = a − b·c^r, where r is the nitrogen rate, a the plateau yield, b the maximum gain and c the curvature (Wortmann et al. 2017). The gain b(1 − c^r) at a given rate reflects water supply, temperature, soil organic matter, texture and acidity, and crop management. Yield variance is large: coefficients of variation above 40% are common and mean yields are 10-30% of rainfed potential (Wortmann et al. 2017). Response varies widely between sites (Nziguheba et al. 2021; Kihara et al. 2016; Rurinda et al. 2020).

**What is measured, and where.** The OFRA database holds about 5,500 response functions from 16 countries (Wortmann et al. 2017); the pilot's August 2023 version has 6,146 function rows at 928 sites. None are in Somalia, Sudan or South Sudan, and of 517 trial site-years since 2010, only 17 had 50 or more violent events within 50 km (15 in Rwanda). The nearest large yield record, HarvestStat Africa (Lee et al. 2025), has 828 administrative units, but units are coarse (Somali districts about 5,700 km2, Sudan states about 57,000 km2), and South Sudan has no usable sorghum or maize yield series after 2012.

**The transfer method and its gap.** The OFRA Inference Tool queries seven raster layers (aridity, temperature seasonality, elevation, distance from the equator, soil pH, sand, organic carbon), selects functions from sites within fixed windows of the query site, and pools them (Wortmann et al. 2017). Its published validation compares pooled pseudo-R2 across domain definitions, not prediction of withheld sites. Spatially clustered data make standard cross-validation optimistic (Ploton et al. 2020), though the right test depends on the use (Wadoux et al. 2021). Treatment effects generalize poorly across settings in general (Vivalt 2020), and hierarchical Bayesian models are the standard way to say how far they travel (Meager 2019).

**The setting.** Sudan and South Sudan together hold millions of farming households: South Sudan about 1.34 million households on 1.26 million hectares of cereals, area up 7% in 2025 (FAO/WFP 2026); Sudan about 15 million hectares of cereals, with 2025 output down 22%. Smallholders in South Sudan use no chemical fertilizer on field crops (FAO/WFP 2026). In Sudan fertilizer is imported, over half of it from the Gulf; one irrigated-scheme farmer reported urea rising from about $11 to about $50 per 50 kg bag between May 2025 and May 2026 (Reuters and AP). Fertilizer use is concentrated in irrigated wheat and vegetables; rainfed sorghum is less fertilizer-dependent except in semi-mechanized systems (Mercy Corps 2026). The best recent yield assessment for Sudan calibrates a satellite index to crop cuts in four states with no held-out test (CIMMYT 2025).

## 2. Research question
**Primary.** How accurately can environmental covariates (soil, climate, Earth-observation vegetation and moisture) predict nitrogen response at unmeasured sites and countries, and how does the error depend on environmental distance from measured sites and on conflict exposure?
**Secondary.** (a) Do satellite time series add information beyond static soil and climate layers? (b) Which locations for new trials most reduce prediction error, given a conflict-risk cap? (c) How does the decision-relevant value of trials change with trial cost and with the fertilizer-to-grain price ratio?

## 3. Hypotheses
Each can fail; criteria are fixed before testing.
- **H1, sufficiency.** Environmental covariates alone do not predict a held-out country's nitrogen response well enough to decide whether fertilizer pays. *Test:* the 80% prediction interval for the gain at 50 kg N/ha excludes break-even (50 kg N × value-cost ratio 2 × price of N ÷ price of grain; 120 kg grain/ha at 2025 prices, 540 kg/ha at 2026 prices) for fewer than half of held-out units. We report the share whichever way it falls. *Pilot expectation:* H1 holds (skill versus pooled curve +0.04 leave-site-out, −0.02 leave-country-out).
- **H2, environmental distance.** Prediction error rises with distance in covariate space from the nearest measured site; once environment is accounted for, conflict exposure adds little (consistent with Karongo et al. 2025 for South Sudan sorghum).
- **H3, coverage design.** Trial sites chosen to cover unsampled covariate space lower held-out error more than the same number of convenient sites.
- **H4, Earth-observation information.** Satellite vegetation (cropland-masked NDVI) and rainfall time series add predictive information beyond static layers. *Pilot expectation:* little across countries (out-of-country R2 0.02-0.07), more within an area (0.15-0.24 within-unit R2).
- **H5, price conditionality.** The share of area cells for which a fertilizer decision can be made falls steeply as the price ratio rises: at data-fitted covariance, 80 trials per country make 16-28% of cells decidable at 2025 prices and 6-9% at 2026 prices; if covariates transfer well (an imposed optimistic bound), about 30 trials reach 42-47% at 2026 prices. *Test:* the Aim 1 estimate of covariance decides which regime holds.

## 4. Methods
### 4.1 Data (all open except ACLED, used under licence)
HarvestStat Africa subnational yields (2010-2023); OFRA response functions; CHIRPS rainfall; MODIS MOD13Q1 250 m NDVI (Earth Engine); TerraClimate aridity; WorldClim temperature seasonality; SRTM elevation; iSDAsoil pH, sand and organic carbon; ACLED violent events (battles, violence against civilians, explosions and remote violence) with fatalities. Each step is scripted with checksums so it can be rerun.

### 4.2 Aim 1: retrospective hold-out (months 1-9, no field data)
Train on all OFRA and HarvestStat data except Somalia; predict Somalia (190 exposed and 322 non-exposed sorghum unit-years; maize 226 and 337). Second hold-out: train before 2020, predict 2020 onward. Models: (a) the Inference Tool as published (selection windows on the seven layers); (b) a hierarchical Bayesian model with site, unit and country effects (Meager 2019); (c) a Gaussian-process model over standardized covariates with Earth-observation time-series features, hyperparameters by marginal likelihood. **Validation:** leave-one-country-out as the headline, spatial and temporal blocking alongside (Ploton et al. 2020; Wadoux et al. 2021). **Metrics:** skill versus a pooled curve (1 − MSE ratio), interval coverage, and the decidable share. **Reproduction check:** reproduce the Inference Tool's published pseudo-R2 using the authors' own rasters (the pilot reproduced it only partially, with proxy rasters). **Output:** error against covariate distance, with and without conflict exposure (H2), and the covariance estimate that decides which H5 regime applies.

### 4.3 Aim 2: proxy assessment (months 6-12)
Test whether conflict-affected places with measurements (north-east Nigeria, the Sahel) predict Somalia and Sudan better than environmental distance alone implies. The pilot finds Somalia's nearest climate neighbours are only slightly farther from it than its own units are from each other (0.19 against 0.14 on standardized rainfall mean and variability) and are rarely conflict-exposed (3%).

### 4.4 Aim 3: prospective trials (months 12-42; funded only if the Aim 1 and price gates below are met)
**Trials.** Replicated nitrogen-response trials on sorghum (maize where grown) at about 30 sites per country: five nitrogen rates, four replicates, plot soil sampling (pH, sand, organic carbon), rain gauges, crop cuts at harvest, and plot geolocation verified by satellite.
**Site selection.** Candidate sites are 0.25-degree cells with an aridity index of at least 0.2. Each cell has a conflict score: violent ACLED events within 50 km over the last 24 months, weighted 1 + ln(1 + fatalities), per year. Under a cap on that score, a greedy algorithm picks the cell that most reduces the area-mean posterior variance of response (the Gaussian-process model of 4.2c), repeated until the budget's trial count is reached. A random subset of convenient sites is the comparison (H3). Pilot: weighted risk of at most 2, 10 and 40 retains 19%, 45% and 80% of Sudan's candidate cells and 23%, 44% and 74% of South Sudan's; information barely differs across caps, so the cap mainly changes cost and feasibility; a cap near 10 is the working choice.
**Cost-and-price frontier (secondary question c).** For each budget B and unit cost c the number of sites is B/c; the model returns the decidable share for each price ratio and cap, and the budget above which an extra thousand dollars adds less than a stated number of decidable cells. Unit costs are parameters: no published per-site cost for fertilizer-response trials in Africa or in conflict settings was found, so partner quotes are required.
**Registered prediction.** Before the first season, Aim 1 predictions and intervals for each trial cell are deposited. Outcome: whether observed response falls in the interval, and how much the interval narrows when trial data are added.

### 4.5 Gates (staged release)
1. **Aim 1 gate:** if covariates beat a pooled curve by little (the pilot says 2-4%), each trial informs mostly its own location and covering a country needs hundreds of trials; the grant then funds a smaller, price-conditional design or a data-collection study. If covariates transfer well, about 30 trials per country achieve most of the gain.
2. **Price gate:** if break-even exceeds the predicted response for the typical unit (at 2026 prices the OFRA mean sorghum response of 476 kg/ha is below the 540 kg/ha break-even), trials target sites predicted to respond.
3. **Access gate:** the conflict cap sets eligibility; trials stop in any cell whose score exceeds it.
4. **Use gate:** before any trial is funded, two or three named users (for example input-supply programs and seed and fertilizer distribution agencies) state what response would change what they buy or distribute.

### 4.6 Risks
Access may close sites: oversample and use verified plot locations. Prediction may prove good (H1 rejected): equally useful, it says where trials can be skipped. Yield records in insecure units may be missing non-randomly: test reporting rates against exposure (no signal in the pilot) and report bounds. Information alone may not change behaviour: a Malawi experiment found a soil-test recommendation had no effect on fertilizer use or yield without a subsidy voucher (Assefa et al. 2026); we therefore frame predictions as an input to *programme targeting* (where scarce fertilizer goes), not as farmer advice.

## 5. Preliminary results (open data; first-pass; not independently reproduced)
1. Somalia can serve as a holdout (counts in 4.2); Sudan sorghum has 65 exposed and 188 non-exposed unit-years; South Sudan none after 2012.
2. Weather carries some signal within a unit and little across countries: within-unit R2 for yield anomalies 0.13-0.19 (rain) and 0.07-0.21 (NDVI) in Somalia and Sudan; out-of-country R2 0.02-0.07; Niger strongly negative (−0.4 to −1.0).
3. The Inference Tool adds little in held-out tests: +0.04 leave-site-out and −0.02 leave-country-out for maize nitrogen at 271 sites with proxy layers; replication of the paper's own table is partial. A Gaussian process over the same covariates gives leave-country-out skill 0.018 and a fitted lengthscale (0.16) far shorter than the distance from Sudan's cells to the nearest OFRA site (median 1.4, against 0.36 between OFRA sites).
4. Trials sit on modestly better land than their surroundings: unfertilized yield a median 1.43 (maize) and 1.26 (sorghum) times the unit yield (94 unit-years; interquartile range reaching 1.0).
5. Trial-size model (section 4.4 and H5): 80 trials per country lower the area-level standard deviation of response from 429 to about 380 kg/ha at data-fitted covariance, and from about 250-290 to about 110-140 kg/ha under the imposed optimistic bound.
6. **What the pilot does not show:** fertilizer response in any conflict-affected country. That absence is why the trials are proposed.

## 6. Timeline and feasibility
Months 1-9 Aim 1; 6-12 Aim 2; gates decided at month 12; 12-42 Aim 3 (three seasons). Aims 1-2 reuse the pilot's open pipeline. Aim 3 depends on in-country partners; none are committed, the USAID-funded CIMMYT programme that produced the 2024/25 assessment may not continue, and costs are not estimated. A budget table would hold: field teams and vehicles, security and insurance, fertilizer and seed, soil and crop-cut analysis, satellite data and compute, partner training.

## 7. Broader impacts
Predictions with honest error bounds help input programmes decide where a trial is needed and where not, and where scarce fertilizer is likely to pay. Candidate users (examples only, none contacted): Sustain Sudan (260,000-tonne discounted fertilizer programme, stated 40% yield-gain assumption), FAO Sudan, Sudan's agricultural financing authorities, and the FAO/WFP assessment missions for South Sudan, which estimate county yields in places they cannot visit. Training of national agronomists in trial and crop-cut design, open code and data.
