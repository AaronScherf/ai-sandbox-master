# NASA Harvest fit, and would J-PAL, CEGA, IFPRI or CIMMYT care? (Addendum v4)

*2026-11-17. Extends [Addendum v3](Feasibility%20Addendum%20-%20Decision-Makers%20for%20the%20Use%20Gate%20-%20v3.md). This is a course proposal: no one has been contacted. "Read" = I read the page; "summary" = search summary only. Interest is my judgement from public pages, not anything anyone told us.*

## 1. Which NASA Harvest call?
There is no single "last Harvest call" to target, and the distinction matters:
- **NASA Harvest is a commissioned consortium** (University of Maryland, NASA Earth Science Division), not a competed grant program. Its Africa initiative works on getting Earth-observation data adopted by national and regional agencies ([Harvest site](https://nasaharvest.org/); summary). A search found no Harvest subaward or open call.
- **The last agriculture-specific ROSES element** was **A.36 Earth Science Applications: Agriculture** (ROSES-2022; notices of intent 8 April 2022, proposals 17 June 2022; [NASA amendment page](https://science.nasa.gov/researchers/solicitations/roses-2022/amendment-7-a36-earth-science-applications-agriculture-final-text-and-due-dates/), read). Its stated goal is a program management capability that increases and enables sustained uses of Earth observations to advance **domestic** agricultural practices; a search summary of the NSPIRES text says international and global agricultural applied research is covered by Harvest. Closed.
- **The last open element listing agriculture** is **ROSES-2025 A.13 Accelerating Earth Solutions** (due 15 October 2026, closed; [NASA page](https://science.nasa.gov/researchers/solicitations/roses-2025/amendment-62-new-opportunity-a-13-accelerating-earth-solutions/), read): three tracks (Needs and Opportunities, Solutions Development, Scaling Impact), awards of one to three years, priority topics framed around US resilience, economic growth, health and security, and more use of AI and machine learning. A ROSES-26 Agriculture element was planned for summer 2026; I found no release.

**Fit and risk.** The NASA-shaped version of this project is *Earth-observation data used in an agricultural decision*, with satellite vegetation and climate data as covariates, a named end user and an application-readiness path. The risks: the applications elements emphasize US benefit, international agriculture is handled through Harvest rather than open competition, and eligibility for non-US sites is not stated on the pages I read. For a course proposal this does not matter; for a real submission the realistic route is a **subaward or partnership with Harvest** (Harvest Africa), with Aim 1 and the Earth-observation work as the NASA-relevant core and the trials funded elsewhere.

## 2. Would J-PAL or CEGA be interested?
- **What they fund.** The Agricultural Technology Adoption Initiative (ATAI), a J-PAL and CEGA collaboration launched in 2009, funds randomized evaluations of programs that increase farmer welfare through productive technologies in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa. Funders: the Gates Foundation, the UK government and an anonymous donor. **It states it is not currently offering a request for proposals**, and eligibility is limited to J-PAL and CEGA affiliated professors and invited researchers ([ATAI page](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/initiative/agricultural-technology-adoption-initiative-atai), read). An old RFP page describes awards up to about $500,000 in two stages (summary; stale).
- **Topical interest.** J-PAL has a named framework for exactly our problem: whether a result from one setting holds in another (Bates and Glennerster, "The Generalizability Puzzle", Stanford Social Innovation Review, 2017; [article](https://ssir.org/articles/entry/the_generalizability_puzzle); no DOI, so not Crossref-checked). They argue for local diagnosis plus mechanisms rather than copying results. Vivalt (2020) and Meager (2019), both in our reference list, come from the same evidence-synthesis tradition.
- **Assessment.** Moderate conceptual interest, low as a funder. Their instrument is the randomized evaluation of a *program*; our project is a *prediction and sampling-design* study with a natural-science covariate model, and the field trials are agronomic response trials, not program evaluations. A fit would require an affiliated professor as co-investigator and a reframing: predicted response as a mechanism-based "local diagnosis" input to a program, with a randomized targeting arm (for example, randomizing whether a distribution program targets by prediction or by convenience) as the evaluation. That is a different, later project.

## 3. Would IFPRI or CIMMYT be interested?
Both are **partners and users, not funders** of external researchers in what I found.
- **IFPRI.** Directly relevant new evidence: [Soil tests, vouchers, and the limits of site-specific fertilizer recommendations: experimental evidence from Malawi](https://cgspace.cgiar.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/40d9ec3e-22e6-4cbc-a3b4-a16b3d79eadb/content) (Assefa, Atkinson, Ayalew, De Weerdt, Siyame, Spielman, Van Campenhout; 15 June 2026; read abstract). A cluster-randomized trial with over 2,000 households in 113 villages: a plot-level soil-test recommendation **alone had no effect** on fertilizer use or maize yields; with a subsidy-equivalent voucher yields rose by roughly one-third, but the value of the extra maize fell short of the voucher's face value, let alone the soil-test cost; voucher recipients bought the standard urea and NPK bundle regardless of the recommendation. IFPRI's Sudan Strategy Support Program (Working Paper 27, read) is also a natural partner. Expect interest in targeting and efficiency, and pushback that **information alone does not change behaviour**.
- **CIMMYT.** Runs SASAS (USAID-funded, November 2022 to November 2025; status afterwards unknown) and is the lead implementing partner of Sustain Sudan. Its own work is close to ours: random-forest site-specific response functions from 6,585 wheat trials in Ethiopia that vary strongly from site to site (summary of [Experimental Agriculture](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/experimental-agriculture/article/datamining-approach-for-developing-site-specific-fertilizer-response-functions-across-the-wheatgrowing-environments-in-ethiopia/2CC4400FF8239FC24373ACBE8CA9071F)), and the Nutrient Expert tool (368 trials; summary). Interest likely in out-of-sample testing of such functions in Sudan; caution about its own USAID-dependent funding.

## 4. What the "value per realistic cost and price" angle requires
The Malawi result sets the bar: even a correct recommendation can fail to pay once the cost of producing it is counted. So the research value of our trials has to be measured against cost and price, not only accuracy. In the pilot model this is already possible and should become an explicit output:
- **Cost axis:** trials per country from a budget B and a unit cost c per site (c is a placeholder; no published conflict-setting figure was found).
- **Price axis:** break-even gain (120 kg grain/ha at $11 a bag; 540 at $50; assumed grain price $0.40/kg).
- **Value axis:** the share of area cells where the 80% interval of response excludes break-even ("decidable"), under a conflict-risk cap.
- **Output:** a frontier of decidable share against budget for each price and cap, plus the budget at which the extra decidable share per dollar falls below a stated threshold. This is a sampling-design question in environmental space (where to sample soil, climate and vegetation conditions), which keeps it natural-science.
Pilot results already show its shape: at data-fitted covariance, 80 trials per country make 16-28% of cells decidable at pre-spike prices and 6-9% at spike prices; with an imposed optimistic covariance, 30 trials reach 42-47% at spike prices.

## 5. Limits
Interest is inferred, not tested. ATAI's silence on RFPs may be stale. The Malawi paper is a working paper (not peer reviewed); I read the abstract and opening pages only. The NSPIRES text for A.36 was seen only through summaries and the NASA amendment page.
