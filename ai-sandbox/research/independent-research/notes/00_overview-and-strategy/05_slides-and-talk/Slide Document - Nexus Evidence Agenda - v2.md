## 1. A planner in a prolonged crisis must choose a design
**Assertion.** Programmes in long-running crises are designed by people choosing among options under constraints, and those choices now reach a large and growing share of the world's poorest people.

**On the slide.**
- The scenario (hypothetical, not a documented portfolio): after repeated drought and displacement, should partners keep short-term transfers, link recipients to a continuing safety net, add livelihood support, or build a system that can scale before the next shock? **[P, from strategy v3 §1]**
- Scale, three facts, each with its source on the slide:
  - A growing share of the world's extreme poor lives in fragile and conflict-affected settings: the World Bank (June 2025) projects roughly 435 million, nearly 60 percent of the total, by 2030 **[S, web search of the press release; verify on the report]**.
  - Forced displacement is long-lasting: UNHCR's 2025 Global Trends reports about seven in ten refugees in protracted situations (exile of five years or more) **[S; verify]**.
  - Humanitarian need is large and persistent: OCHA's 2026 Global Humanitarian Overview counts about 239 million people needing aid **[S; verify]**.

**Why it is here.** It answers "why does this matter", at the scale of lives and money, before any research question. It also sets up the point of the talk: each of those people is reached through a design choice someone made.

**Evidence status.** The three figures come from a web search summary, not from the primary documents. Verify before Friday and keep the source and year on the slide. The share of the poor in fragile settings **depends on the poverty line used** (a Center for Global Development analysis finds it much lower at a higher line) **[S]**, so say "projected" and name the line, or use the qualitative statement and put the figure in the speaker note. Bedoya et al.'s abstract gives an older version of the same point (extreme poverty fell from 36 percent in 1990 to 10 percent in 2015 but is rising in fragile settings) **[C, from the abstract you pasted]**.

**Speaker note.** One sentence of scale, then move on. The aim is the next slide's question, not the statistics.

**Do not say.** "Most of the world's poor"; the older two-thirds projection; that the figures show planners lack evidence (they show the stakes).

## 2. The nexus, who decides, and how much room they have
**Assertion.** The "nexus" is where short-term aid and long-term development decisions overlap, and the people who decide work inside limits set by donors, headquarters and rules.

**On the slide.**
- Definition (my wording, not sourced): *coordinating short-term life-saving aid with longer-term development where shocks repeat or crises last for years* **[I]**. A citable alternative is the OECD DAC recommendation on the humanitarian–development–peace nexus (cited in strategy v3; I have not read it).
- Distinguish: immediate response (the setting of most decision-making studies) versus programme design in protracted or repeated crises (this agenda).
- **Planner discretion** defined: the choice that remains after rules, budget, mandate and norms. It is an overlooked fact on the ground; recommendations that ignore it tend to fail, for example the limited uptake of cash transfers despite the evidence **[O]**.
- Stakeholders (reprokit principle: research made with an audience in mind): field planners; headquarters and central research offices; evidence-generation funders; researchers; donors and governments. One line each on what they decide and what they would get **[P, v9 §4]**.

**Why it is here.** The audience is not from the sector. It fixes the vocabulary and shows who the work is for.

**Evidence status.** The definition is mine. The cash-uptake example comes from your reasoning and from the evidence-use scans **[S]**; if asked for a source, the CALP and Barder et al. material in the adversarial scan is the pointer, unchecked.

**Do not say.** That discretion is small or large in general; it is what RQ1 measures.

## 3. Evidence is worth seeking only if it could change the choice
**Assertion.** The value of evidence depends on three things: room to choose, uncertainty about which option is best, and evidence that is relevant enough to move the planner's belief.

**On the slide.** The three conditions; relevance has two parts, *distance* (will it hold here?) and *actionability* (can this planner implement it?). The three-row decision table: relevant and usable, so use it; relevant but too distant, so transport it; none, so generate or substitute. The full 2 by 4 table (with planner discretion yes or constrained) goes to the appendix.

**Why it is here.** It gives economists and environmental scientists their own language (expected value of information; the same logic as adaptive management and decision-making under deep uncertainty) and turns "do planners use evidence?" into "in what share of decisions would more evidence matter?".

**Evidence status.** Value of information in health economics is established (Claxton 1999; Fenwick et al. 2008) and has been applied to grant allocation (Brown, Salisbury and Ball 2025) **[C for Brown; others in the project set]**. Hjort et al. (2021): mayors valued research and a research session raised policy adoption **[C]**.

**Do not say.** That decisions fall neatly in cells; the table is a simplification of a continuum.

## 4. What exists already: a mixed picture

## 5. Motivating cases: evidence exists, does not decide, and travels unevenly
**Assertion.** Three documented cases show the problem the talk is about: plentiful evidence did not decide a real choice (Kenya HSNP); two cash studies in Kenya report different local-economy spillovers and we cannot yet say why; and one programme worked in a conflict setting where earlier evidence came from stable ones (Bedoya et al.).

**On the slide.** Three short panels, no figures.
- **Kenya HSNP Phase 2:** seven expansion options that differed in scale, speed and financing; one recommended; the evidence supported all expanded options and did not speak to the choice planners faced. A drought case, and the options differ in scale, not intervention type.
- **Two Kenyan cash studies:** a randomized trial of one-time transfers in rural Kenya (Egger et al.) and the HSNP Phase 2 local-economy evaluation both report spillovers on the local economy, with different results. The measures are defined differently and are not directly comparable, so **why they differ is not established**. Both are on the replication list as a test of how far evidence travels.
- **Bedoya et al. (Afghanistan):** a graduation programme tested in a conflict setting after success in stable ones; pre-registered; large effects reported; no replication data found. The aspirational case: evidence can travel, and the question is what made it possible.

**Why it is here.** The cases come before the research questions so the audience sees the three problems first: supply does not meet the decision, results differ across settings, and success despite conflict is possible. It merges the old slides 5 and 10.

**Evidence status.** Kenya **[C]** against the Business Case and Completion Review (Kenya check v1); log-frame, options weighting tables and the HSNP evaluations not read. Egger versus HSNP is a **hypothesis-generating pair, not a documented transport failure [C]**: one is an import-adjusted expenditure multiplier, the other income per shilling in four northern counties, and the Completion Review does not attribute the gap to a contextual factor. The methods sections are not yet read (transcription in progress). Bedoya: abstract and contribution statement checked **[C, from the text you pasted]**; the full paper is yours to read tonight. n is small and the cases were chosen for documentation, not sampled.

**Speaker note (numbers stay off the slide).** Egger multiplier about 2.5; HSNP regular-transfer LEWIE 0.38 to 0.93 income per shilling; Bedoya consumption up 30 percent. Replication status: Egger package public (not run by me); HSNP microdata in the folder; Bedoya registered (Social Science Registry 2665), no data. About three minutes; cut Kenya to two lines if time is short.

**Do not say.** That one result "failed to transport"; that Kenya is a conflict case; that the three cases are a sample.

## 6. The mismatch is one of relevance, not price: three questions
**Assertion.** Evidence and research attention are non-market goods, so what gets studied can be misallocated; the gap is mostly relevance to the decision, and it can be studied in three linked questions.

**On the slide.** The market-failure sentence; the three RQs in one line each:
- **RQ1:** how much discretion do planners have on a decision, and how do they value the evidence available?
- **RQ2:** what do their plans rest on, and is the barrier searching for evidence or overload?
- **RQ3:** can the barriers be reduced (search, synthesis, finding the closest comparable settings) and can the predictions be tested?
Goal: more successful interventions, judged by whether pre-registered predictions turn out accurate **[O]**.

**Why it is here.** It is the spine; everything after is one of the three questions. It also states the contribution to research allocation (slide 13).

**Evidence status.** Framing and wording are yours and mine **[O/I]**; v9 §3 and Abstract v2.

## 7. RQ1: measure discretion and the value placed on evidence
**Assertion.** Whether more evidence would change decisions is measurable, starting from documents and without human subjects.

**On the slide.** The four value-of-information questions (is there discretion left after constraints; can existing information already separate the options; does the timeline allow more; who bears the cost of being wrong) **[P, v3 §3.1]**. The first instrument is a **desk audit** of a documented programme, including whether decision-makers sought outside support for evidence and under what conditions (revealed demand) **[O, v4 §4]**. Later: a documented partner funnel, treated as data **[O, v5 §1]**. Headquarters versus field constraints, and decisions about where to generate evidence, are in scope.

**Why it is here.** It makes the first research question concrete and shows the first step is feasible now.

**Evidence status.** Instruments are proposals. The taxonomy of decisions (immediate response, long-term planning, operational, design) is **not settled** and needs more work before any audit **[O]**. The headquarters/field relationship is the same.

**Do not say.** That we know the share of decisions in any cell.

## 8. RQ2: what plans rest on
**Assertion.** Planners' plans rest on context data, tacit knowledge, best practice and standard procedures; their causal maps, scenarios and log-frames carry assumptions, stated or not, and getting to the best decision runs into a search problem or an overload problem.

**On the slide.** The four bases of plans; "assumptions in a log-frame" as the unit of study; search cost versus overload, and what each would look like in a document trail.

**Why it is here.** It links the audit of discretion to what planners actually do with evidence, and it keeps the earlier work on constraints and log-frames visible.

**Evidence status.** v9 redefined RQ2 compared with v3–v5 (constraints, scenarios, thresholds). Decide whether the old log-frame example becomes the illustration here (owner choice, process audit §3i). No precedent found for participatory elicitation of causal maps with practitioners; Tennant et al. is **not** one **[C]**.

## 9. RQ3: how far does a finding travel? What counts as comparable
**Assertion.** Whether a result transfers depends on how the source and target differ, and "comparable" has to be defined along named dimensions.

**On the slide.** The dimensions: spatial (including environment), temporal, institutional, market, treatment version, population and interference **[P, v3 §3.2]**. The idea: an insecure region in one country may resemble an insecure region in another more than the rest of its own country, so conflict zones could learn from each other with a synthetic-control-style comparison **[P]**. "What counts as comparable" is stated as the open design problem.

**Why it is here.** It is the core of the transfer question and the answer to "why not just apply the result?".

**Evidence status.** General argument: Pritchett and Sandefur (2015), transport error larger than local bias in most of their cases **[C]**. Transport theory, bounds (Gechter) and site selection (Montiel Olea et al.) exist **[S]**. The definition of "comparable" is under investigation **[O]**.

## 10. The pipeline already under construction: the 2×2
**Assertion.** The first steps toward the causal transport model are a set of partly automated, open-source, reproducible tools. Some are built and some are planned. The aim is a proof of concept for a pipeline that can be re-run on any decision, not a one-off study; the dissertation validates it on one applied case.

**On the slide.** The 2×2 with a status tag in each cell, `reprokit` underneath, and the planned replication cases.

| | **1. Find and describe what is known** | **2. Test what can be used here** |
|---|---|---|
| **Evidence** | **1.1 Discovery, synthesis and extraction.** Literature search, synthesis, and structured extraction of study facts, including causal claims **[confirm what stage 2 extracts]**. *Built and piloted.* | **2.1 Evidence coverage and gap analysis.** Where studies resemble the target choice and where they do not (3ie taxonomy). *In design: the evidence-map repo exists; the study catalogue comes first.* |
| **Local and replicable** | **1.2 Context data.** Climate, conflict, prices, displacement at defensible scales. *Built: the Somalia and Sudan panel rebuilds with matching hashes.* | **2.2 Reproducible evaluations.** Extract and replicate eligible studies. *Framework released (`reprokit` v0.4.0); first replication (Bangladesh insurance) waiting on village coordinates; Kenya cash studies planned.* |

- **`reprokit`:** every output carries recorded hashes and rebuilds in continuous integration, so a result can be re-run by someone else.
- **Cycle:** decision, search, context audit, eligible comparison, conditional inference, feedback from use.
- **Replication candidates:** Egger et al. and the HSNP Phase 2 evaluation (the test case on slide 5); Bangladesh insurance (Hill et al.); Bedoya et al. if data can be found.
- **Honest scale:** the quadrants are separate assets; linking them is the plan, not a finished system. The replication quadrant is the slow, gated one.

**Why it is here.** Existing infrastructure is one of the strongest parts of the talk, and it frames the transport model as the next step on a path, not a leap.

**Evidence status.** Status per the projects tracker **[C]**: panel built and reproducing, `reprokit` released, evidence map in brainstorming, transferability planned with no repo, data-fusion toolkit deferred. ## 11. The causal transport model: approaches and context data
**Assertion.** The transport model is the methodologically open part. Several approaches exist, each needs particular data, and remote sensing gives a way to describe context at scale.

**On the slide.** Two columns.
- **Candidate approaches** (a menu, not a choice):
  - *Selection diagrams* (Pearl and Bareinboim): state which source–target differences matter and whether an effect is identifiable at all.
  - *Reweighting* on measured modifiers, when source and target overlap.
  - *Partial-identification bounds* (Gechter): bounds or a sign when overlap fails.
  - *Partial pooling* across studies to model heterogeneity, with predictions from priors, pre-registered.
  - *Cross-country comparison of insecure regions* in a synthetic-control style.
  - *Site selection* (Montiel Olea et al., a k-median problem): which studies to replicate next.
  - *Structural breaks* (conflict, displacement, market closure) as triggers to re-audit.
- **Context data:**
  - *Remote sensing:* rainfall (CHIRPS, already in the panel); vegetation indices (NDVI, EVI) from Landsat (from 2013), MODIS and Sentinel-2 (from 2015); night lights.
  - *Other:* conflict events (ACLED), market prices (WFP), displacement (DTM), boundaries and population.
  - *Limits:* the resolution has to suit the unit (Landsat pixels suit smallholder plots, MODIS can blur them); coordinates for the study sites are the decisive gate; satellite data describe environment and activity, not treatment or welfare.
- **Output:** a stated prediction type (estimate, bounds, sign or uncertainty), pre-registered with an informativeness criterion and a power calculation.

**Why it is here.** This is the methodologically most interesting part and where the environmental-science half of the audience comes in. Satellite treatment-effect comparison on the Kenya cash trial already exists (Huang et al.), so there is prior art to build on.

**Evidence status.** Pritchett and Sandefur on why distance matters **[C]**. Gechter and Montiel Olea were read by Gemini only **[S]**. Pearl and Bareinboim are from my knowledge and not in the project's checked set **[I, unverified]**. The heterogeneity point is from Vivalt, cited in strategy v3. Sensor start dates and resolution are catalog facts from the trial-eligibility note and do not show usable cloud-free observations over any given village **[C of the note]**. No approach has been shown to work in crisis settings.

**Do not say.** That remote sensing identifies treatment effects; that any approach is validated here.

## 13. Why it matters beyond this case
**Assertion.** If the questions are answered, the benefits reach research in several fields as well as planners.

**On the slide (one line each).**
- **Development economics:** a way to say beforehand how far an estimate travels, not only that external validity is a worry.
- **Decision science and value of information:** the first application of the framework to programme-design decisions with measured discretion, rather than to whether to fund an evaluation.
- **Causal inference:** transport methods tested prospectively, with pre-registered predictions, in a hard setting.
- **Climate and environmental decision-making:** a decision-relevance test for scenario and adaptation methods used under deep uncertainty (for example robust decision-making).
- **Research funding and evidence synthesis:** separating genuine research gaps from those that can be filled by adapting existing evidence, to guide where funders commission new studies.
- **Research infrastructure:** an open-source, reproducible pipeline for evidence search, synthesis and replication that others can re-run on a different decision.
- **Humanitarian and nexus practice:** a documented map of what plans rest on, and where central rules block use of evidence.

**Why it is here.** It answers "why does this matter" for the research audience, complementing the scale on slide 1.

**Evidence status.** These are intended contributions, **[P]** and not results. Each depends on the corresponding RQ working. Do not present as achieved.
