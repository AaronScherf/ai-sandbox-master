## 1. A planner in a prolonged crisis must choose a design
**Assertion.** Programmes in long-running crises are designed by people choosing among options under constraints, and those choices now reach a large and growing share of the world's poorest people.

**On the slide.**
- The scenario (hypothetical, not a documented portfolio): after repeated drought and displacement, should partners keep short-term transfers, link recipients to a continuing safety net, add livelihood support, or build a system that can scale before the next shock? **[P, from strategy v3 §1]**
- Scale, three facts, each with its source on the slide:
  - A growing share of the world's extreme poor lives in fragile and conflict-affected settings: the World Bank (June 2025) projects roughly 435 million, nearly 60 percent of the total, by 2030 **[S, web search of the press release; verify on the report]**.
  - Forced displacement is long-lasting: UNHCR's 2025 Global Trends reports about seven in ten refugees in protracted situations (exile of five years or more) **[S; verify]**.
  - Humanitarian need is large and persistent: OCHA's 2026 Global Humanitarian Overview counts about 239 million people needing aid **[S; verify]**.

**Why it is here.** It answers "why does this matter", at the scale of lives and money, before any research question. It also sets up the point of the talk: each of those people is reached through a design choice someone made.

**Evidence status.** The three figures come from a web search summary, not from the primary documents. Verify before Friday and keep the source and year on the slide. The share of the poor in fragile settings **depends on the poverty line used** (a Center for Global Development analysis finds it much lower at a higher line) **[S]**, so say "projected" and name the line, or use the qualitative statement and put the figure in the speaker note. Bedoya et al.'s abstract gives an older version of the same point (extreme poverty fell from 36 percent in 1990 to 10 percent in 2015 but is rising in fragile settings) **[C, from the abstract you pasted]**.

## 2. The nexus, who decides, and how much room they have
**Assertion.** The "nexus" is where short-term aid and long-term development decisions overlap, and the people who decide work inside limits set by donors, headquarters and rules.

**On the slide.**
- Definition (my wording, not sourced): *coordinating short-term life-saving aid with longer-term development where shocks repeat or crises last for years* **[I]**. A citable alternative is the OECD DAC recommendation on the humanitarian–development–peace nexus (cited in strategy v3; I have not read it).

- **Planner discretion** defined: the choice that remains after rules, budget, mandate and norms. It is an overlooked fact on the ground; recommendations that ignore it tend to fail, for example the limited uptake of cash transfers despite the evidence **[O]**.
- Stakeholders (reprokit principle: research made with an audience in mind): field planners; headquarters and central research offices; evidence-generation funders; researchers; donors and governments. One line each on what they decide and what they would get **[P, v9 §4]**.

**Why it is here.** The audience is not from the sector. It fixes the vocabulary and shows who the work is for.

## 3. Evidence is worth seeking only if it could change the choice
**Assertion.** The value of evidence depends on three things: room to choose, uncertainty about which option is best, and evidence that is relevant enough to move the planner's belief.

**On the slide.** The three conditions; relevance has two parts, *distance* (will it hold here?) and *actionability* (can this planner implement it?). The three-row decision table: relevant and usable, so use it; relevant but too distant, so transport it; none, so generate or substitute. The full 2 by 4 table (with planner discretion yes or constrained) goes to the appendix.

**Why it is here.** It gives economists and environmental scientists their own language (expected value of information; the same logic as adaptive management and decision-making under deep uncertainty) and turns "do planners use evidence?" into "in what share of decisions would more evidence matter?".

**Evidence status.** Value of information in health economics is established (Claxton 1999; Fenwick et al. 2008) and has been applied to grant allocation (Brown, Salisbury and Ball 2025) **[C for Brown; others in the project set]**. Hjort et al. (2021): mayors valued research and a research session raised policy adoption **[C]**.

**Do not say.** That decisions fall neatly in cells; the table is a simplification of a continuum.

## 4. Where this sits in the literature
**Assertion.** The problem is a hard case of questions that several familiar fields already ask. Those fields offer methods and findings we can build on; none addresses a programme-design decision in a prolonged crisis, where evidence is thin and distant. If the approach works there, it should extend to fields with more and closer evidence.

**On the slide.** A five-row map: the field, what it gives us, what it leaves open here.

| Field | What it gives us | What it leaves open here |
|---|---|---|
| **Generalizing from impact evaluations** (development economics) | Effects vary across settings and implementers (Vivalt 2020); transport error can exceed local bias (Pritchett and Sandefur 2015); sites are not representative (Allcott); hierarchical models of cross-site variation (Meager); scaling changes interventions (Banerjee et al.; Bold et al.) | Mostly well-studied interventions in stable settings; few comparable sites in crises |
| **Transportability and data fusion** (causal inference) | When an effect can be carried from one population to another, and what must be measured (Bareinboim and Pearl); bounds when it cannot (Gechter); which sites to study (Montiel Olea et al.) | Tested rarely on real programme decisions, and not from the planner's own causal view |
| **Evidence use and evaluation uptake** (policy studies, mostly qualitative) | Why evidence is or is not used: barriers, relationships, timing (Oliver et al. 2014; Hjort et al. 2021 on policymakers valuing research) | Seldom measures, decision by decision, how much discretion exists or how relevant the evidence is |
| **Evidence synthesis and gap maps** (3ie) | Evidence gap maps show which interventions and outcomes have studies (Snilstveit et al. 2016) | Show where studies are, not whether they are relevant to a particular decision and context |
| **Decisions under uncertainty, including climate and disaster response** | Value of information (Claxton; Brown et al. 2025); robust decision-making and adaptive pathways for deep uncertainty (Kwakkel, Haasnoot and Walker 2016); evaluations of anticipatory action and adaptive social protection | Value of information is applied to funding choices, not to whether existing evidence already settles a design choice; climate methods rarely audit the causal evidence they rest on |

- **Nearest humanitarian work:** IDinsight's value-of-information tool (whether to commission an evaluation), ALNAP (immediate-response decisions) and Elrha (barriers to evidence use). These are the closest prior art found; none audits existing evidence against a nexus design decision.
- **Why start here:** the hard case has the least evidence and the most constraint, so a method that works here should carry over to health, education, agriculture and climate adaptation, where evidence is plentiful and closer.
- A footnote: the examples in this talk come from the best-documented sector (cash and livelihoods).

**Why it is here.** It places the work among fields the audience knows, instead of presenting a narrow gap, and it shows the use case is one instance of a general method.

**Evidence status.** Identifiers for Vivalt (2020), Allcott (2012 working paper), Meager (2017 preprint), Banerjee et al. (2016 working paper), Oliver et al. (2014), Snilstveit et al. (2016) and Kwakkel et al. (2016) were matched against Crossref **[C, registry match, not read]**. Pritchett and Sandefur and Hjort et al. **[C]** from the project's transcripts. Bold et al., Gechter, Montiel Olea et al., Brown et al. (read), IDinsight, ALNAP and Elrha come from earlier checks; the reading of the last three is Gemini's **[S]**. Bareinboim and Pearl is registry-matched for a 2012 report only. ## 6. The mismatch is one of relevance, not price: three questions

## 9. RQ3: how far does a finding travel? What counts as comparable
**Assertion.** Whether a result transfers depends on how the source and target differ, and "comparable" has to be defined along named dimensions.

**On the slide.** The dimensions: spatial (including environment), temporal, institutional, market, treatment version, population and interference **[P, v3 §3.2]**. The idea: an insecure region in one country may resemble an insecure region in another more than the rest of its own country, so conflict zones could learn from each other with a synthetic-control-style comparison **[P]**. "What counts as comparable" is stated as the open design problem.

**Why it is here.** It is the core of the transfer question and the answer to "why not just apply the result?".

**Evidence status.** General argument: Pritchett and Sandefur (2015), transport error larger than local bias in most of their cases **[C]**. Transport theory, bounds (Gechter) and site selection (Montiel Olea et al.) exist **[S]**. The definition of "comparable" is under investigation **[O]**.

**Working definition to test [P].** "Comparable" is a *profile*, not a single score. For each dimension in the list above (and each link of the planner's causal map), record two kinds of measure:
- **Objective:** a measured distance between the source and target settings, for example whether the average vegetation index (NDVI) of the two areas has a similar distribution, or how close their market-access, conflict-exposure or delivery-system indicators are.
- **Subjective (expert belief):** the planner's rating of similarity on that dimension on a fixed scale, for example "forest health: the source area is a 4 on a 6-point similarity scale".
Settings are comparable for a decision when, on the dimensions the planner's map says matter, both kinds of measure fall inside thresholds the planner accepts. A gap between the objective and the subjective measure on a dimension is itself flagged for discussion. The source and target maps must share variables for this to work: the source study's own causal story is matched to the target's map first (slide 11).

## 10. The pipeline already under construction: the 2×2

The aim is a proof of concept for a pipeline that can be re-run on any decision, not a one-off study; the dissertation validates it on one applied case.

**On the slide.** The 2×2 with a status tag in each cell, the loop between the cells, and the planned replication cases.

| | **1. Find and describe what is known** | **2. Test what can be used here** |
|---|---|---|
| **Evidence** | **1.1 Discovery, synthesis and extraction.** Automated literature search, synthesis, and structured extraction of study facts, including causal claims **[confirm what stage 2 extracts]**. *Built and piloted.* The extracted causal claims and mechanisms give each source study's own causal map, which is matched to the planner's map on slide 11 **[P]**. | **2.1 Evidence gap map.** Following the **3ie evidence gap map** (an intervention by outcome matrix of where studies exist) and extending it with context: shock type, crisis phase, delivery institution, displacement, horizon. *In design: the evidence-map repo exists; the study catalogue comes first.* |

## 11. The causal transport model: built with the decision-maker, not around them
**Assertion.** The transport model is not a black-box predictor trained in isolation. It starts from the decision-maker's own causal map of the programme (RQ2); the planner judges whether a source is close enough on the links that matter; and the answer comes back translated into terms the planner can check. The process runs under a written protocol so that the feedback does not bias the result.

**On the slide.**

*The expert-in-the-loop cycle:*
1. **RQ2 supplies the target map.** The planner's causal chain from activities to outcomes, the blockers and assumptions in the log-frame, and the thresholds that would force a different choice.
2. **Match the maps.** The source study's causal story (from the extracted causal claims in cell 1.1) is matched to the target map, so both use the same variables. Links that exist in one map and not the other are flagged.
3. **Flag the links that differ.** This is a *selection diagram*: the causal graph (a DAG), with a mark on each link that works differently in source and target (payment system, market access, security, delivery institution).
4. **Measure and judge.** Context data measure each flagged link (objective distance); the planner rates the same dimension (subjective similarity); together they form the comparability profile (slide 9). The planner decides which differences are tolerable and which rule a source out.
5. **Compute a prediction** of one of four types (below).
6. **Translate to the planner's terms, and check.** The prediction is scaled to the area, population and hypothetical scale of the intervention, to give a target metric the planner can judge ("about this many households and this change in consumption"). The planner says whether it is reasonable, corrects the map, and the cycle repeats.

*Four types of prediction, depending on what the evidence supports:*

| Type | When |
|---|---|
| **Point estimate with standard errors** | Maps match, distance small, enough comparable studies |
| **Bounds on the impact** (Gechter) | Overlap is partial; the sign or range can be bounded |
| **Ordinal category or sign** (positive, negative, none) | Only direction is supported |
| **"Insufficient evidence" or "distance too great"** | No defensible transport; this is a result, and it routes to the data-insufficient gate |

*Four approaches to compute it, in three groups, still to be explored and compared:*

| Group | Approach | What it does | Where the expert enters |
|---|---|---|---|
| **What can be carried over?** | **1. Selection diagrams** | Shows which differences must be measured and whether a transport formula exists | The graph comes from the planner's map; the planner flags the differing links |
| **How to combine evidence** | **2. Hierarchical Bayesian models** | Treat each site's effect as drawn from a distribution; predict a new site's effect with uncertainty (Meager) | Priors and which sites count as exchangeable |
| **How to combine evidence** | **3. Similarity-weighted borrowing** | Weight source sites by closeness to the target on the dimensions that matter: reweighting and synthetic-control-style weights | The planner's map decides which variables, and how close is close enough |
| **What if it cannot?** | **4. Partial-identification bounds** (Gechter) | Report bounds or a sign when overlap or assumptions fail | The planner supplies the assumptions the bounds rest on |

- **Design choices around the approaches (not methods):** which settings count as comparable (for example insecure regions in different countries); which studies to replicate next (k-median site selection); a trigger to re-audit when a shock changes the setting.
- **Context data:** remote sensing (rainfall such as CHIRPS, already in the panel; vegetation indices from Landsat, MODIS and Sentinel-2; night lights) and conflict events, prices, displacement, boundaries. Limits: satellites describe environment and activity, not treatment or welfare; resolution has to suit the unit; site coordinates decide what can be linked.
- **Guarding against bias from the feedback rounds:** a written protocol fixes the steps (data discovery, modelling, planner review rounds, repeat), what the planner may change, how every change is logged, and when predictions are locked. Details in the appendix.

**Why it is here.** It makes the key design commitment explicit: "distance" is defined by an expert-validated causal map and judged by the person who will use the answer, not by a generic similarity score; and the answer is translated into a quantity that person can test against their own knowledge. The Egger versus HSNP pair is the first test: do the four approaches agree on whether a result carried from one setting to the other?

**Evidence status.** The expert-in-the-loop design and the protocol are proposals **[P]**. The RQ2 products it uses (log-frames per scenario, per-node data inventory and decision thresholds, the relevance check before the transport stage) are in v3 §2 and v4 §5 **[C]**. The four output types are the output tiers from strategy v3 and v3 §2, with Gechter bounds as the second **[C]**. Selection diagrams are Bareinboim and Pearl's formalism; my plain-language description is **[I]**. Systems mapping is an established field; its link to DAGs for transport is what is new and untested **[owner; I]**. Approaches 2 and 3 are standard ideas whose use here is untested; synthetic control proper builds a counterfactual for one treated unit from a panel, so "synthetic-control-style" borrows the weighting idea only **[I]**. Gechter and Montiel Olea were read by Gemini only **[S]**. Sensor dates and resolutions are catalog facts from the trial-eligibility note and do not show usable cloud-free observations over any village. No approach has been shown to work in crisis settings.

**Speaker notes.** The sentence to land: "The model does not decide what is close enough. The person who will act on it does, using a map they helped draw, and the answer comes back in numbers they can check." If asked what a selection diagram is: a cause-and-effect diagram where you mark the arrows that work differently in the two places. If asked about bias: "the process is fixed in a protocol before we start, and every change is logged" (appendix).

**Do not say.** That any approach is validated here; that remote sensing identifies treatment effects; that cross-country comparison is a method; that the model replaces the decision-maker's judgment; that the feedback loop is bias-free.

## Appendix note: protocol and bias controls for the feedback rounds [P]
The risk: because the variables and distance judgments are refined with the planner, someone will ask how this avoids choosing the specification that gives the answer people like (p-hacking, researcher or planner degrees of freedom). The process helps accuracy, but it needs rules. Proposed elements, to be refined and registered:
1. **A written protocol before the first case**, registered like the predictions: the steps (data discovery, map matching, comparability profile, modelling, planner review round, repeat), the stopping rule for the cycle, and what the planner may and may not change.
2. **Constrain what is open.** Fix in advance the dimensions to be profiled (the transfer dimensions), the candidate approaches (the four), and the form of each output; leave open only the choice of variables within each dimension, from a documented candidate list.
3. **Log every round.** Every variable considered, every map change, every planner rating and why; versioned so the history can be audited.
4. **Blind the review to the estimate where possible.** The planner validates the maps and rates similarity before seeing the predicted effect, then reviews the translated target metric separately; changes after seeing the estimate are flagged as such.
5. **Show the sensitivity.** Report the prediction under the alternatives considered (all variable sets tried, not only the chosen one), the multiverse of reasonable specifications.
6. **Lock before the outcome.** The final prediction is time-stamped and pre-registered before implementation, as already planned, so the evaluation is a clean test of whatever came out of the cycle.
7. **Test the process, not only the prediction.** Compare predictions from the planner-guided specification against a fixed, planner-free baseline; if the guidance does not improve out-of-sample accuracy, say so.
8. **Be explicit that the planner's rating is a belief, not a measurement**, and report the spread across more than one expert where possible.
This is a proposal; the right controls depend on the registry and design, and on whether the cases allow a planner-free comparison.
