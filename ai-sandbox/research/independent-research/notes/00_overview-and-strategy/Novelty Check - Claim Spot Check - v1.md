## 2. Erroneous "Increased" Statements in Summary v4 and RQ3 v4

The transcript of Crost, Felter, & Johnston (2016, *JDE*, `1-s2.0-S0304387815000966-main`, p. 1) explicitly proves that conditional cash transfers **decreased** conflict incidents and reduced insurgent influence in treated villages. The following locations in the existing v4 notes erroneously state "increased" (conflating this finding with Crost et al.'s 2014 *AER* paper on CDD infrastructure projects) and must be corrected:

1. **`Novelty Check - Summary - v4.md` (Line 71):**
   * *Existing text:* `| **Cash in Conflict** | CCTs in the Philippines increased insurgent violence due to rebel sabotage incentives | Crost, Felter, & Johnston (2016) | [REGISTRY-MATCHED] |`
   * *Required correction:* Change "increased insurgent violence due to rebel sabotage incentives" to "decreased conflict incidents and insurgent influence in treated villages (though displacement to control villages could not be ruled out)".
2. **`Novelty Check - RQ3 Transfer - v4.md` (Line 58):**
   * *Existing text:* `*Violence Escalation and Strategic Extortion:* Crost, Felter, & Johnston (2016) demonstrated experimentally in the Philippines that conditional cash transfers increased insurgent violence during rollout, as rebels sabotaged infrastructure to prevent government loyalty gains.`
   * *Required correction:* Change to reflect that Crost et al. (2016) found cash transfers *decreased* insurgent violence and influence (whereas the sabotage/escalation finding was from Crost et al. 2014 on community-driven infrastructure, KALAHI-CIDSS).

## 4. Analytical Comparison: RQ1 Contribution vs. Brown et al. (2025)

Brown, Salisbury, & Ball (2025, *AEA Papers and Proceedings*, 115: 308–312) represents the closest published prior art applying Value of Information (VoI) principles to philanthropic development capital. However, their contribution differs fundamentally in institutional scope, decision architecture, and operational reality from Aaron's proposed RQ1 knowledge audit:

Brown et al. evaluate **centralized, high-level capital allocation decisions for a global philanthropic foundation (GiveWell)**, formalizing whether to spend millions of dollars commissioning multi-year primary RCTs (e.g., a \$3.4M trial of ORS/zinc distribution in Nigeria) versus directing capital directly to known cost-effective charity grants (such as bed net distributions). Their simplified VoI framework rests on global health parameter uncertainty, subjective Bayesian priors, and an explicit assumption of *post-trial certainty* across decade-long scaling horizons. Crucially, Brown et al. **do not address humanitarian or acute crisis settings, frontline program-design parameter choices (such as transfer modality, targeting thresholds, transfer sizing, or delivery logistics), or rapid decision windows where running a new trial is impossible**. Furthermore, while GiveWell's model decides *whether to commission future academic research*, it omits any structured protocol for *auditing existing internal and external evidence* against rigid operational constraints. Aaron's RQ1 contribution operates at the completely unaddressed frontline interface: adapting VoI not as a capital-allocation tool for funding multi-year RCTs, but as a **rapid operational knowledge audit and decision-intake protocol** that screens whether retrieving external evidence can marginally shift a binding, fast-closing program design choice in protracted or acute crises, and formally establishing the **"value of no additional research"** when frontline administrative, political, or supply constraints bind.
