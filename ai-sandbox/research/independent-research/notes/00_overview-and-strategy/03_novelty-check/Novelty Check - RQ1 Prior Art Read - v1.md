## 1. Document (a): IDinsight Technical Blog (March 2022)

* **Citation:** Allier-Gagneur, Z., Devlin-Foltz, S., Sobti, M., & Shotland, M. (2022). *Improving decisions about evidence: IDinsight's new Value of Information work*. IDinsight Blog, 28 March 2022.  
* **Source:** Fetched directly from IDinsight (`https://www.idinsight.org/article/improving-decisions-about-evidence-idinsights-new-value-of-information-work/`). Full HTML text audited.  
* **Epistemic Tag:** `[FULL-TEXT-READ]`

### Audit Questions & Verbatim Evidence

1. **Does the tool cover programme-design choices or only commission-an-evaluation-or-not?**
   * **Finding:** The tool strictly addresses **commissioning an evaluation or not**. It evaluates whether spending funds on a study reduces "decision risk" enough to justify diverting resources from program implementation, specifically informing high-stakes binary or scale decisions (whether to scale up, discontinue/defund, or reallocate funds). It does **not** model or optimize operational programme-design parameter choices (such as transfer modality, targeting cutoffs, transfer sizing, or logistical delivery mechanisms).
   * **Verbatim Quotes (< 25 words):**
     * *"Picture a decision-maker who is deciding whether to increase the scale of a program... we want to be confident that the evaluation will improve decisions enough to justify the cost."* (Section: Introduction, 23 words)
     * *"Does the simulated study lead the decision-maker to change their scale decision?"* (Section: Step 2, 11 words)
     * *"They also tell us how much money a decision-maker would, on average, correctly reallocate after investing in a particular study."* (Section: Step 2, 18 words)

2. **Any humanitarian or crisis example?**
   * **Finding:** **None.** The blog contains zero examples from humanitarian aid, acute conflict, disaster response, or refugee crises. The illustrative examples discuss generic development programs, sanitation data collection (*"An enumerator collects data on sanitation from a respondent"*), and public education.

3. **Any step that audits existing evidence first?**
   * **Finding:** **No systematic step audits existing evidence or literature first.** Step 1 focuses exclusively on eliciting the decision-maker's subjective *prior beliefs* and quantifying baseline "decision risk" in the absence of a study. The authors observe informally that clarifying constraints or beliefs can sometimes reveal that an evaluation is unnecessary (e.g., when administrative constraints leave only one option), but they provide no structured literature intake, evidence mapping, or knowledge audit protocol.
   * **Verbatim Quotes (< 25 words):**
     * *"First, we estimate how likely it is that a decision-maker is choosing an incorrect option in a world where we don't conduct a study."* (Section: Step 1, 21 words)
     * *"if political or administrative constraints mean that a decision-maker has only one choice... it may not be that helpful to measure its impact precisely"* (Section: Why we're excited, 21 words)

## 2. Document (b): ALNAP / ODI Operational Decision-Making Study (2019)

* **Citation:** Campbell, L., & Knox Clarke, P. (2019). *Beyond Assumptions: How humanitarians make operational decisions*. ALNAP/ODI, London. (108 pp).  
* **Source:** Fetched full PDF directly via ReliefWeb (`ALNAP beyond assumptions study web.pdf`, 2,550,267 bytes, 108 pages). Full text extracted and audited.  
* **Epistemic Tag:** `[FULL-TEXT-READ]`

### Audit Questions & Verbatim Evidence

1. **Exact Title:**
   * **Finding:** *"Beyond Assumptions: How humanitarians make operational decisions"* (Cover / p. 1). Authors: Leah Campbell and Paul Knox Clarke.

2. **The "3.4% of decisions referenced research" figure:**
   * **Exact Verbatim Quote (< 25 words):**
     > *"Only 3.4% of the submitted decisions which sought information mentioned looking for best practice/lessons/research efficacy data."* (p. 43, 18 words)
   * **Definition:**
     The 3.4% figure does **not** mean that 3.4% of all operational decisions cited research. Rather, it represents the subset of decisions where decision-makers actively sought external information, of which only 3.4% sought documented lessons, best practice, or research efficacy data ("the hard data – often described as 'evidence'"). The authors emphasize that operational humanitarian decision-makers overwhelmingly rely on social information sources (discussions with colleagues, field teams, headquarters, and trusted informants) and structured operational needs/targeting assessments. Furthermore, many operational choices (e.g., HR decisions, partner selection, security posture) are relational rather than efficacy-driven, rendering academic efficacy evidence irrelevant to practitioners in real time.
   * **Sample:**
     A mixed-methods empirical study conducted across **8 operational humanitarian response contexts** (including Bangladesh, Ukraine, Kenya, Nigeria, Turkey, and Lebanon). A total of **55 country-level decision-makers** (60% INGO, 25.5% NNGO, 7% UN, 5.5% Red Cross/Red Crescent; 58% international staff, 42% national staff) logged real-time operational decisions using a structured mobile app diary (CrowdLab). The cohort submitted a total of **1,035 real-time decisions**, with 32 decision-makers completing the full study by logging 16+ decisions each and completing two in-depth qualitative follow-up interviews (pp. ii, 14, 43).

## 3. Document (c): Elrha Learning Paper (2022)

* **Citation:** Smith, G., Swithern, S., & Harrison, K. (2022). *From knowing to doing: Evidence use in the humanitarian sector*. Elrha, London. (64 pp).  
* **Source:** Fetched full PDF directly from Elrha (`elrha-r2hc-from-knowing-to-doing-learning-paper.pdf`, 1,062,380 bytes, 64 pages). Full text extracted and audited.  
* **Epistemic Tag:** `[FULL-TEXT-READ]`

### Audit Questions & Verbatim Evidence

1. **Main finding on why evidence goes unused:**
   * **Finding:** Identifies four intractable, systemic barriers embedded in the political economy of both the humanitarian sector and academia:
     1. *Time pressures:* The structural mismatch between short humanitarian funding/response cycles and long academic research/publishing timelines.
     2. *Funding constraints:* Short grant cycles, rigid donor budgets, and lack of dedicated funding to implement evidence-based operational changes.
     3. *Lack of relevance to humanitarian practice:* Academic research questions formulated without practitioner input, resulting in evidence that is abstract, overly complex, or disconnected from frontline operational realities.
     4. *Lack of relevance to Global South actors:* Power imbalances and Northern institutional dominance in research leadership, undermining local ownership and contextual fit.
   * **Verbatim Quotes (< 25 words):**
     * *"We find four basic barriers to using research evidence: time pressures; funding constraints; lack of relevance to humanitarian practice; and lack of relevance to humanitarian actors in the Global South."* (Executive Summary p. 6; Findings p. 19, 24 words)
     * *"The mismatch of time between urgency of humanitarian decision-making and the long time frame for producing robust research findings... may be an unavoidable tension"* (p. 23, 23 words)

2. **Any method for deciding when evidence is worth seeking?**
   * **Finding:** **None.** The Elrha report diagnoses institutional barriers, reviews evidence-brokering mechanisms (such as the International Rescue Committee's Outcomes and Evidence Framework, oef.rescue.org), and advocates for implementation science and localized research agendas. However, it proposes **no formal method, decision rule, economic stopping rule, or quantitative framework** for determining when evidence is worth seeking versus when operational constraints bind. In fact, while noting that humanitarians have developed informal quality-vs-speed trade-off frameworks for rapid needs assessments (p. 11), the authors explicitly state that they *"will not adopt any hierarchical evidence-ranking framework"* (p. 11), leaving the determination of when evidence is actionable or worth the search cost unaddressed.

## 4. Synthesis: Implications for Aaron's RQ1 Contribution

Reading these three core prior art documents in full establishes the exact operational and methodological void that Aaron's RQ1 (Knowledge Audit & Value of Information) addresses:

1. **The Capital vs. Operations Void (IDinsight):**
   IDinsight's VoI tool operates as an *evaluation commissioning model* for high-level development actors deciding whether to pay for a new multi-month study before scaling a stable national program. It does not touch operational programme design (modality, targeting thresholds, transfer sizing), provides no intake screening for existing evidence, and completely ignores humanitarian and crisis settings.
2. **The Empirical Reality of Evidence Neglect (ALNAP):**
   Campbell & Knox Clarke (2019) document empirically that efficacy research is sought in only 3.4% of information-seeking operational decisions in crises. This neglect is not mere negligence: frontline decisions are heavily relational, procedural, and bounded by acute time constraints where academic research is perceived as absent or unusable.
3. **The Diagnostic vs. Method Gap (Elrha):**
   Smith et al. (2022) thoroughly diagnose *why* evidence goes unused (time mismatches, funding rigidity, lack of operational relevance), but offer *no actionable decision protocol* for practitioners to determine whether seeking evidence is worth the search cost.

**Aaron's Contribution:** A structured, rapid **Knowledge Audit protocol** that operates directly within the frontline humanitarian decision window: screening binding political, logistical, and administrative constraints *before* evidence retrieval; evaluating whether external evidence can marginally alter the pending program-design choice; and formalizing the **"value of no additional research"** as an explicit, defensible decision deliverable when constraints bind.
