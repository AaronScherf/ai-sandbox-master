## 1. Verdict

**Verdict: Partly answered.**

The formal mathematical apparatus of Value of Information (Expected Value of Perfect Information [EVPI] and Expected Value of Sample Information [EVSI]) is well developed in Bayesian decision theory and extensively operationalized in health economics and clinical trial design. In international development and humanitarian aid, however, VOI has primarily been applied either to physical weather forecast triggers for anticipatory action or to philanthropic grant allocations between direct aid and evaluations, while field studies of policymaker demand have tested willingness to pay for research summaries rather than formal preposterior value. Operationalizing VOI as an intake and screening tool to audit a crisis program planner's existing knowledge, assess whether marginal evidence justifies collection delays and costs, and track partner outreach as an observable conversion funnel remains completely unaddressed in published research.

## 2. Findings on Sub-Questions

### Sub-Question 1: Has value-of-information analysis (EVPI/EVSI) been applied to decisions about commissioning evidence in humanitarian or development programme design?
* **Category Tag:** **(c) Method exists elsewhere (health economics; environmental management; weather forecasting), but not in this setting.**
* **Findings:**
  * *Health Economics:* The transfer of classical Bayesian decision theory (Raiffa & Schlaifer, 1961; Howard, 1966) to research commissioning was pioneered in health technology assessment by Claxton (1999) and Claxton & Sculpher (2006). They established that research should be commissioned only when the Expected Value of Sample Information exceeds the Expected Cost of Research, explicitly treating policy adoption without further trial as an alternative to clinical evaluation. Fenwick, Claxton, & Sculpher (2008) extended this to separate the value of additional research from the value of implementation activities.
  * *Anticipatory Action / Forecast-Based Financing:* In disaster risk reduction and humanitarian early action, decision-analytic valuation models have been applied to *physical forecast triggers* rather than evidence commissioning (e.g., Coughlan de Perez et al., 2015; Lopez et al., 2020). These models weigh the cost of "acting in vain" (false alarms) against the cost of inaction (unmitigated disaster losses) to calibrate probabilistic flood or drought thresholds. They do not evaluate commissioning research or adapting external causal evidence for program design.
  * *Philanthropic Grant Allocation:* In development economics, Brown, Salisbury, & Ball (2025, *AEA Papers and Proceedings*) describe how GiveWell uses a value-of-information framework to allocate approximately $10 million annually between commissioning new randomized controlled trials and funding direct program delivery. Their framework calculates the expected probability of updating beliefs and shifting future philanthropic capital. However, this is an investor-level portfolio framework for global grantmaking, not an operational decision-support tool for in-country humanitarian program planners deciding whether to adapt an intervention.
  * *Status for Humanitarian / Transition Programme Design:* No published framework exists applying formal EVPI, EVSI, or Expected Net Benefit of Sampling (ENBS) to guide an operational agency or humanitarian desk officer on whether to commission a study, run a pilot, or proceed with existing knowledge under recurrent crisis constraints.

### Sub-Question 2: Existing frameworks for auditing a decision-maker's evidence needs and evidence use
* **Category Tag:** **(b) Related question answered.**
* **Findings:**
  * *Evidence-to-Decision (EtD) Frameworks:* The GRADE Working Group developed Evidence-to-Decision frameworks for clinical practice and public health policies (Alonso-Coello et al., 2016; Moberg et al., 2018). These provide structured checklists covering problem priority, magnitude of benefits/harms, certainty of evidence, resource use, equity, acceptability, and feasibility. While comprehensive, GRADE EtD assumes synthesized systematic review evidence already exists; it does not compute marginal information value or audit whether searching for more evidence is worth the time in an acute window.
  * *Decision-Focused Evaluation:* Organizations such as IDinsight (Shah et al., 2015; Abraham et al.) have operationalized "Decision-Focused Evaluations" (DFEs) and "Decision-Driven Data" in international development. DFEs begin from a specific upcoming decision (e.g., scale-up, pivot, terminate) and tailor evaluation timing and precision thresholds to that choice. However, DFEs remain consulting-driven heuristic engagements rather than formal decision-theoretic audits with explicit stopping rules.
  * *Humanitarian Evidence Uptake and Decision Realities:* ALNAP's empirical study of humanitarian decision-makers (*Beyond Assumptions*, Campbell & Knox Clarke, 2019) observed that only 3.4% of recorded operational decisions referenced research or formal evidence, with naturalistic (recognition-primed) and procedural heuristics dominating time-constrained choices. Similarly, Elrha's synthesis (*From Knowing to Doing*, Smith et al., 2022) identified operational time limits, funding inflexibility, and institutional mandates as primary barriers to evidence uptake. These studies diagnose why evidence is neglected, but do not provide an actionable diagnostic audit protocol for planners.
  * *Evidence Readiness Frameworks:* Tools like the NESTA Standards of Evidence (Puttick & Ludlow, 2012) and the Pew-MacArthur Results First clearinghouse classify the maturity of evidence supporting an intervention (Levels 1 to 5), but do not audit the decision-maker's specific context, decision space, or information absorption capacity.

### Sub-Question 3: Studies of policymakers' or practitioners' demand for or willingness to pay for research, and consultant commissioning
* **Category Tag:** **(b) Related question answered.**
* **Findings:**
  * *Experimental Demand for Evidence:* Hjort, Moreira, Rao, & Santini (2021, *American Economic Review*) conducted field experiments with 2,150 Brazilian mayors and municipal officials. Using Becker-DeGroot-Marschak (BDM) mechanisms, they proved that policymakers have a positive, substantial willingness to pay (WTP) for research evidence (valuing larger sample trials more than smaller ones). Furthermore, providing research summaries increased the adoption of an effective policy by 10 percentage points.
  * *Belief Updating and Cognitive Biases:* Vivalt & Coville (2023, *Journal of Development Economics*) surveyed over 100 policymakers from institutions like the World Bank and Inter-American Development Bank. They found that policymakers update beliefs asymmetrically—updating significantly more on "good news" (positive impact estimates) than on "bad news"—and exhibit "variance neglect" by ignoring confidence intervals and dispersion across studies.
  * *Commissioning of External Consultants:* In public administration and humanitarian logistics, the decision to hire external consultants or think tanks (e.g., Humanitarian Outcomes, OPM) is driven largely by organizational legitimacy, donor-mandated third-party evaluation requirements, and temporary surge capacity rather than calculated information optimization (e.g., Baaij, 2022; Mazzucato & Collington, 2023; Sturdy, 1997). ALNAP's 2019 findings confirm that operational staff frequently view formal consultant evaluations as retrospective compliance rituals rather than inputs into forward-looking program redesign.

### Sub-Question 4: Response and uptake rates for researcher-practitioner partnership outreach
* **Category Tag:** **(d) Nothing found for cold outreach conversion in development/humanitarian partnership formation; (b) related in political science audit studies.**
* **Findings:**
  * *Audit and Correspondence Studies of Public Officials:* In political science and public administration, experimental audit studies measuring civil servant or legislator response rates to unsolicited inquiries typically report response rates between 15% and 50% (e.g., Butler & Broockman, 2011; Costa, 2017; Coppock, 2014). Response rates vary significantly based on perceived constituent status, organizational prestige, and perceived workload.
  * *Research-Practice Partnerships (RPPs):* The literature on research-practice partnerships in education and social policy (Henrick et al., 2017; Coburn & Penuel, 2016; Farrell et al., 2021) documents that successful RPPs almost never arise from cold outreach. They require extensive relationship-building, institutional memoranda of understanding, and shared governance. 
  * *Humanitarian Partnership Funnel Data:* There are no published empirical benchmarks documenting conversion rates for unsolicited researcher outreach offering evidence-adaptation support to humanitarian or transition agencies (approached → responded → declined → accepted → judged fit). The owner's planned documentation of this funnel constitutes a novel empirical contribution to the political economy of evidence uptake.

## 3. What Aaron's Contribution Could Still Be

Given that the core concepts exist in adjacent domains (health economics, municipal administration experiments) but remain absent from crisis program adaptation, an original PhD contribution for RQ1 could consist of:

1. **A Practical Decision-Theoretic Knowledge Audit Protocol:**
   Translating formal VOI principles into a rapid, structured intake instrument for prolonged-crisis program adaptation. Instead of full-scale Monte Carlo EVSI simulations, the tool would evaluate:
   * *Residual Discretion:* Do institutional mandates, security, and earmarked funding leave open choices?
   * *Separability:* Can existing operational data and known evidence separate the surviving options?
   * *Temporal Feasibility:* Can external evidence or a new pilot be completed before the decision window closes?
   * *Reversibility and Stakes:* What is the expected loss from implementing the suboptimal option?
2. **Empirical Funnel Benchmark:**
   Systematically documenting the outreach funnel for humanitarian and transition partners as an observable measure of revealed demand for external research support. Categorizing reasons for non-response or refusal (e.g., lack of discretionary authority, time poverty, distrust of external metrics, compliance focus) provides primary empirical data on where the evidence pipeline breaks before research even begins.
3. **The "Value of No Additional Research" as an Explicit Deliverable:**
   Formulating a defensible stopping rule that formally demonstrates to a humanitarian agency when spending additional funds on needs assessments or external evidence synthesis has zero marginal value because constraints bind or existing data suffice.
