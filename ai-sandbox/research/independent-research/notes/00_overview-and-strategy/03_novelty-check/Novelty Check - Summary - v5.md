## 2. Key Substantive Insights and Novelty Boundaries

### Insight 1: Value of Information is Established for Global Funders, But Unapplied to Frontline Crisis Design
While the mathematical foundations of Value of Information (EVPI and EVSI) have been refined for decades in Bayesian decision theory and health economics (Claxton, 1999; Fenwick et al., 2008), its application to international development has only recently emerged. Where it has appeared—such as GiveWell’s philanthropic grant-allocation model (Brown, Salisbury, & Ball, 2025) and IDinsight’s simulation-based EVSI framework (Allier-Gagneur et al., 2022)—it has been formulated for **high-level, multi-year evaluation commissioning decisions** (e.g., whether to fund an RCT before scaling a stable national health program).

In contrast, **no literature adapts formal VOI to the operational program design workflow of frontline humanitarian and transition managers**. Humanitarian agencies still treat evidence acquisition either as an unconditioned moral priority or as a rigid compliance checkbox, rather than an economic commodity whose value is strictly bounded by whether it can change an operational decision before a rapid decision window closes. Developing a rapid knowledge audit protocol that screens residual discretion and establishes formal stopping rules represents a significant, unaddressed contribution.

### Insight 2: The Methodological Chasm Between Participatory Systems Mappers and Causal Econometricians
Every prominent participatory causal mapping method utilized with policy practitioners—Participatory Systems Mapping (PSM; Barbrook-Johnson & Penn, 2022), Causal Loop Diagrams (CLDs), and Fuzzy Cognitive Maps (Kok, 2009)—prioritizes **feedback loops, circularity, and mutual causality**. In contrast, formal causal transportability theory (Pearl & Bareinboim, 2014; Dahabreh et al., 2020) relies fundamentally on **Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs)** to achieve d-separation, back-door adjustment, and identification of causal effects across heterogeneous populations.

While cyclic structural causal models exist mathematically (e.g., Bongers et al., 2021, *The Annals of Statistics*) using $\sigma$-separation and solvability conditions, this remains a pure mathematical statistical theory of equilibrium states that does not address qualitative stakeholder elicitation. In parallel, systematic reviews of empirical health literature (Tennant et al., 2021) demonstrate wide inconsistencies in how researchers construct and report DAGs, while participatory workshops in development overwhelmingly produce static cyclic loops that cannot identify causal effects. Developing a practical elicitation protocol that guides non-technical planners to express dynamic feedback as time-indexed, identification-compliant DAGs is an open, high-value methodological bridge.

### Insight 3: Pre-Registration Exists for Replication Models and Human Forecasts, But Not for Causal Transport Predictions in Crises
Pre-registration has become standard practice across social science, encompassing both human expert predictions (e.g., forecasts recorded on the Social Science Prediction Platform; DellaVigna, Pope, & Vivalt, 2019, *Science*) and statistical models predicting binary lab replication outcomes (Altmejd et al., 2019, achieving 70% accuracy). Furthermore, econometricians frequently evaluate transportability retrospectively on historical multi-site datasets (e.g., leave-one-site-out tests on Opower or microcredit; Allcott, 2015; Meager, 2019; Gechter, 2024).

However, **no published study in development or humanitarian aid has calibrated an ex-ante causal transport model (or partial identification bounds) using external evidence and target baseline data, pre-registered numerical predicted treatment effects and bounds for an unstudied crisis target population, and prospectively evaluated those predictions against a newly fielded field trial**.

Furthermore, a cross-cutting investigation into Evidence and Gap Maps (EGMs; White, Saran, & Welch, 2020) revealed that despite dozens of published gap maps across humanitarian and development sectors, **none have operationalized quantitative stopping rules or capture-recapture saturation metrics (such as Kastner et al., 2009; Brok et al., 2008)**. EGMs classify matrix cells as "gaps" purely based on retrieved studies, leaving researchers unable to formally distinguish whether an empty cell reflects true absence of evidence or an underpowered search.
