5.4 Relations among the basic families. The named distributions are easier to remember when organized by the experiments that generate them. Bernoulli: This describes a single success or failure trial. Binomial: This counts the number of successes in a fixed number of independent Bernoulli trials. Geometric: This measures the waiting time until the very first Bernoulli success. Negative binomial: This measures the waiting time until the r -th Bernoulli success. Poisson: This counts the number of arrivals in a fixed interval, assuming a constant-rate arrival model. Exponential: This measures the waiting time to the first Poisson arrival. Gamma: This measures the waiting time to the r -th Poisson arrival, specifically when the shape parameter is an integer. For small success probabilities, the binomial distribution has a Poisson approximation. Consider a random variable, X sub n, that follows a Binomial distribution with n trials and a success probability of p sub n. As the product of n and p sub n approaches a constant value, lambda, the probability that X sub n equals k approaches a specific value. This value is e to the power of negative lambda, multiplied by lambda to the power of k, all divided by k factorial. This approximation holds for k values of zero, one, two, and so on. Thus, Poisson counts naturally arise as a limit for rare events. Now, if a random variable X follows an Exponential distribution with a rate parameter of lambda, its survival function is given by the following: The probability that X is greater than a specific value x is equal to e raised to the power of negative lambda times x. The memoryless property of the Exponential distribution follows immediately from this. The probability that X is greater than s plus t, given that X is already known to be greater than s, is calculated by taking e to the power of negative lambda times the quantity s plus t, and dividing it by e to the power of negative lambda times s. This expression simplifies directly to e to the power of negative lambda times t. The geometric distribution is the discrete analogue of this memoryless property.

5.6 Convolution. If X and Y are independent random variables, each having its own density function, then their sum, S, which equals X plus Y, also has a density. This density, denoted as f sub S of s, is given by the integral from negative infinity to positive infinity of f sub X of x, multiplied by f sub Y of s minus x, all with respect to x. This expression is also equivalent to the convolution of f sub X and f sub Y, evaluated at s. In essence, convolution is the distributional counterpart of adding independent random variables. Let's consider Example 5.2: The Sum of Independent Exponentials. Suppose X and Y are independent and identically distributed, following an exponential distribution with parameter lambda. Then, for any s greater than zero, the density function of X plus Y, evaluated at s, is found by taking the integral from zero to s of the product of two terms: first, lambda times e to the power of negative lambda x, and second, lambda times e to the power of negative lambda times the quantity s minus x, all integrated with respect to x. This calculation simplifies to lambda squared times s times e to the power of negative lambda s. Therefore, X plus Y follows a Gamma distribution with parameters two and lambda. This demonstrates how repeated convolution can lead to the gamma distribution, which is often used to model the waiting time for multiple Poisson arrivals.

5.7 Moment-generating and characteristic functions. For a random variable that takes non-negative integer values, the probability generating function is G sub X of s, which equals the expected value of s to the power of X. This also equals the sum from k equals zero to infinity of s to the power of k, multiplied by the probability that X equals k. This holds for values of s where the series converges. It packages the entire probability mass function and is especially convenient for sums and branching or counting models. For independent random variables X and Y, both taking non-negative integer values, the probability generating function of their sum, G sub X plus Y of s, equals G sub X of s multiplied by G sub Y of s. For example, the probability generating function for a binomial distribution with parameters n and p, G sub Bin of n comma p of s, equals the quantity one minus p plus p s, all raised to the power of n. And for a Poisson distribution with parameter lambda, G sub Pois of lambda of s, equals the exponential of the quantity lambda times s minus one. The moment-generating function is M sub X of t, which equals the expected value of e to the power of t X. This is defined where the expected value is finite. If it exists on an open interval around zero, it determines the distribution, and the k-th derivative of M sub X with respect to t, evaluated at t equals zero, equals the expected value of X to the power of k. For independent random variables X and Y, the moment-generating function of their sum, M sub X plus Y of t, equals M sub X of t multiplied by M sub Y of t. The characteristic function is phi sub X of t, which equals the expected value of e to the power of i t X. Unlike a moment-generating function, it always exists because the absolute value of e to the power of i t X is always equal to one. It uniquely determines the law. For independent sums, the characteristic function of the sum, phi sub X plus Y of t, equals phi sub X of t multiplied by phi sub Y of t.
