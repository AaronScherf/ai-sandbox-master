10 Stochastic Processes: Martingales and Markov Chains. Sources. This bridge chapter follows Williams and Durrett for martingales and Markov chains, with MIT 6.436 as a model for how these objects naturally follow a first rigorous probability course. This chapter is not necessary for a minimal probability block, but it provides the natural bridge to macroeconomics, finance, and dynamic econometrics.

10.1 Stochastic processes and filtrations. A stochastic process is a family of random variables, X sub t, indexed by t belonging to a set T, all defined on a common probability space. A filtration, represented by a sequence of nested sigma-algebras—F sub zero, contained within F sub one, which is contained within F sub two, and so on—serves to represent the accumulation of information over time. A process is considered adapted if the random variable X sub t is measurable with respect to the sigma-algebra F sub t.

10.2 Martingales. Here's the passage rewritten for audio narration: "Definition 10.1: Martingale. An integrable, adapted process, which we denote as M sub t, is a martingale with respect to the filtration F sub t, if the following condition holds: The expected value of M at time t plus one, given the information available up to time t (which is F sub t), is equal to M at time t, almost surely. For instance, consider a sequence of random variables, X sub t, that are independent and identically distributed with a mean of zero. If we define M sub t as the sum of X sub s, where s ranges from one up to t, then M sub t is a martingale under what is known as the natural filtration. Another important concept is a martingale difference sequence, denoted as u sub t. This sequence satisfies the condition that the expected value of u at time t, given the information available up to time t minus one (F sub t minus one), is equal to zero. If this condition is met, then the process M sub t, defined as the sum of u sub s, from s equals one up to t, is a martingale. This is the time-series equivalent of a regression error that has a mean of zero, conditional on all past information."

10.3 Markov chains. A discrete-time process, X sub t, is defined as Markov if the probability of its state at the next time step, t plus one, falling within a specific set A, when we know its entire past history – its state at time t, time t minus one, and all prior states – is identical to the probability of it being in set A at time t plus one, when we only know its current state at time t. For a finite number of possible states, we use a transition matrix, P. The entry in row i and column j of this matrix, denoted P sub i j, represents the probability that the process will move to state j at time t plus one, given that its current state at time t is i. If we represent the probabilities of being in each state at time t as a row vector, called pi sub t, then the probabilities for the next time step, pi sub t plus one, can be calculated by multiplying the current probability vector, pi sub t, by the transition matrix P. A stationary distribution, represented by the vector pi, is defined by the equation where pi equals pi multiplied by the transition matrix P. Provided the Markov chain is finite, irreducible, and aperiodic, the distribution of states will eventually converge to a single, unique stationary distribution.

10.4 Stationarity and an ergodic LLN. A process, which we denote as X sub t, is strictly stationary if the joint distribution of a vector of random variables, X at time t1 through X at time tk, is equal in distribution to the joint distribution of that same vector, but with each time index shifted by h. So, X at time t1 plus h, through X at time tk plus h. This must hold for every finite collection of dates and every admissible shift h. Stationarity alone does not imply that time averages converge to population means. An additional ergodicity condition is needed to rule out persistent random components that never average away. We now turn to Remark 10.2, titled "Rigorous Layer: Birkhoff’s Ergodic Theorem, Probability Form." For a stationary and ergodic process where the expected value of the absolute value of X at time zero is finite, the sample average of X sub t, taken from t equals 1 to T, converges almost surely to the expected value of X at time zero. This theorem is the dependent-data analogue of the strong law, and it's most directly relevant for stationary time series and dynamic economic models. Next, we have Remark 10.3, titled "Why This Belongs at the End." The earlier chapters provide all the necessary ingredients: sigma-algebras describe information, conditional expectations describe forecasts, and convergence theorems describe long-run behavior. Martingales and Markov chains simply organize these ingredients over time.

11 Further Distributional Tools for Economists. Sources. This section fills several gaps between the standard univariate distribution material and the multivariate calculations used in econometrics. The organization follows the emphasis in Hong on joint transforms, implications of independence, and sampling distributions, with Hansen as a complementary economics-facing reference.

11.1 Joint moment-generating and characteristic functions. For a random vector, let's define X as a column vector with elements X sub 1 through X sub k. Its joint moment-generating function, M sub X of t, is given by the expected value of e raised to the power of t transpose X, where t is a vector in k-dimensional real space. This is defined whenever the expectation is finite in a neighborhood of the origin. The joint characteristic function, on the other hand, is phi sub X of t, which is the expected value of e raised to the power of i times t transpose X, again where t is a vector in k-dimensional real space. This function always exists. The joint transform contains both marginal and cross-moment information. For example, the partial derivative of M sub X of t with respect to t sub j, evaluated at t equals zero, equals the expected value of X sub j. And, when the second moments exist, the second partial derivative of M sub X of t with respect to t sub j and t sub L, evaluated at t equals zero, equals the expected value of the product X sub j times X sub L. Thus, the covariance matrix can be recovered from the Hessian of the joint moment-generating function at the origin. Now, let's consider Proposition 11.1, which discusses factorization under independence. If we have random variables X sub 1 through X sub k that are independent, and their individual moment-generating functions exist near zero, then M sub X of t sub 1 through t sub k equals the product from j equals 1 to k of M sub X sub j of t sub j. An analogous factorization also holds for characteristic functions, and this is true without any moment assumptions: phi sub X of t sub 1 through t sub k equals the product from j equals 1 to k of phi sub X sub j of t sub j. Conversely, factorization of the joint characteristic function into the marginal characteristic functions implies independence. For Example 11.2, illustrating independent Poisson counts and their sum: Suppose we have the following: X follows a Poisson distribution with parameter lambda, Y follows a Poisson distribution with parameter mu, and X and Y are independent. In this case, their joint moment-generating function is M sub X comma Y of s comma t, which equals the exponential of lambda times the quantity e to the s minus 1, multiplied by the exponential of mu times the quantity e to the t minus 1. If we set both s and t equal to u, this gives us the moment-generating function of the sum X plus Y: M sub X plus Y of u equals the exponential of the quantity lambda plus mu, all multiplied by the quantity e to the u minus 1. From this, we can conclude that X plus Y follows a Poisson distribution with parameter lambda plus mu.

11.2 Linear combinations, portfolios, and covariance accounting. Consider any random vector, X, which exists in k-dimensional real space, and has a covariance matrix we'll call Sigma. Also consider any deterministic vector, 'a', which also exists in k-dimensional real space. In this scenario, the variance of the dot product of 'a' and 'X' is equal to 'a' transposed, multiplied by Sigma, multiplied by 'a'. Expressed in terms of individual coordinates, the variance of the sum, from j equals one to k, of 'a sub j' times 'X sub j', is equal to the sum, from j equals one to k, of 'a sub j' squared, times the variance of 'X sub j'. This is then added to two times the sum, for all j less than l, of 'a sub j' times 'a sub l', times the covariance of 'X sub j' and 'X sub l'. The terms involving covariance disappear if the variables are pairwise uncorrelated, but this is not because variance itself is a linear operation. Here's an example, number eleven point three: a two-asset portfolio. Let R sub p be equal to 'w' times 'R sub one', plus the quantity 'one minus w' times 'R sub two'. Then, the expected value of R sub p is equal to 'w' times the expected value of R sub one, plus the quantity 'one minus w' times the expected value of R sub two. And the variance of R sub p is equal to 'w' squared times 'sigma one' squared, plus the quantity 'one minus w' squared times 'sigma two' squared, plus two times 'w' times the quantity 'one minus w' times 'rho' times 'sigma one' times 'sigma two'. Diversification is therefore a statement about covariance, as well as about individual variances. If 'rho' is less than one, an interior portfolio can have a lower variance than a simple weighted average of the two individual variances.

11.3 The F distribution and ratios of quadratic forms. The normal, chi-square, t, and F distributions form a tightly connected family. Definition 11.4: The F distribution Let Q sub one be distributed as a chi-square distribution with nu sub one degrees of freedom, and Q sub two be distributed as a chi-square distribution with nu sub two degrees of freedom. Furthermore, let Q sub one be independent of Q sub two. Then, the variable F is defined as the ratio of Q sub one divided by nu sub one, to Q sub two divided by nu sub two. This F variable has an F distribution with nu sub one and nu sub two degrees of freedom. This is often written symbolically as F is distributed as F with nu sub one and nu sub two degrees of freedom. If a variable T is distributed as a t distribution with nu degrees of freedom, then T squared is distributed as an F distribution with one and nu degrees of freedom. This identity explains the equivalence between a two-sided t test and a one-restriction F test in the Gaussian linear model. If two independent normal samples satisfy the following: X sub one through X sub m are independently and identically distributed as a normal distribution with mean mu sub X and variance sigma sub X squared; and Y sub one through Y sub n are independently and identically distributed as a normal distribution with mean mu sub Y and variance sigma sub Y squared, then the ratio of S sub X squared divided by sigma sub X squared, to S sub Y squared divided by sigma sub Y squared, is distributed as an F distribution with m minus one and n minus one degrees of freedom. Thus, the F distribution appears naturally when comparing independent variance estimates.

11.4 Mixture distributions and latent heterogeneity. Economic populations are often heterogeneous. A convenient probability model introduces a latent group variable, which we'll call G. Suppose that the probability of the latent group variable G taking on a specific value, little g, is equal to pi sub g. And the sum of all these pi sub g values, for g ranging from one up to capital G, is equal to one. And conditional on G being equal to little g, the variable X has a cumulative distribution function, F sub g, and a density function, little f sub g. Then the unconditional law is the mixture. The cumulative distribution function of X, at a value x, is equal to the sum, from g equals one to capital G, of pi sub g multiplied by F sub g of x. And similarly, the density function of X, at x, is equal to the sum, from g equals one to capital G, of pi sub g multiplied by little f sub g of x. The law of iterated expectations gives us the expected value of X. This is equal to the sum, from g equals one to capital G, of pi sub g multiplied by mu sub g. Here, mu sub g is defined as the expected value of X, conditional on G being equal to little g. And the law of total variance gives us the variance of X. This is equal to the sum, from g equals one to capital G, of pi sub g multiplied by sigma sub g squared. Plus the sum, from g equals one to capital G, of pi sub g multiplied by the quantity mu sub g minus the unconditional mean mu, all squared. Hence, aggregate variation decomposes into within-group and between-group variation. Example 11.5 — A two-type income mixture Let's consider Example 11.5, a two-type income mixture. Suppose that X, conditional on G being equal to zero, is distributed as a normal distribution with a mean of zero and a variance of one. And X, conditional on G being equal to one, is distributed as a normal distribution with a mean of three and a variance of one. Furthermore, the probability that G equals one is equal to pi. Then, the expected value of X is equal to three times pi. And the variance of X is equal to one plus nine times pi times the quantity one minus pi. Even though each type has variance one, latent heterogeneity raises the unconditional variance through the between-type component. Remark 11.6 — Mixtures need not inherit the shape of their components A mixture of normal distributions is generally not normal. It may be skewed, heavy-tailed, or multimodal. This is one reason why aggregate cross-sectional distributions can look very different from the conditional distributions faced by homogeneous subgroups.

12 Sampling Distributions and Finite-Sample Calculations. Sources. Hong's sampling chapter emphasizes the distinction between a population, a random sample, a statistic, and the statistic's sampling distribution. This section makes that distinction explicit and collects the finite-sample calculations that students use repeatedly before asymptotic theory takes over.

12.1 Population, random sample, statistic, and sampling distribution. Here is the passage rewritten for audio narration: Definition 12.1: Sampling distribution Let X sub one, through X sub n, be independently and identically distributed according to F. This represents a random sample from a population law F. And let T sub n be a statistic, defined as a function T of X sub one, through X sub n. The sampling distribution of T sub n is the probability law induced on T sub n by repeated random sampling from F. The population law F describes individual observations. The sampling distribution describes a function of an entire sample. These are different objects. For example, even when the individual observations, X sub i, are Bernoulli, meaning X sub i belongs to the set containing zero and one, the sample mean, X bar sub n, belongs to the set containing zero, one over n, and so on, up to one, and has a different distribution. A statistic is random before observing the data and numerical after observing the data. Thus, X bar sub n is a random variable ex ante, whereas a realized sample produces a specific number, lowercase x bar sub n.

12.2 Exact sampling distribution of a sample proportion. Let X sub one, through X sub n, be independently and identically distributed random variables, each following a Bernoulli distribution with parameter p. We then define p-hat as the sample mean, X-bar sub n. It follows that n times p-hat, which is equivalent to the sum of all X sub i values, follows a Binomial distribution with parameters n and p. Consequently, the probability that p-hat equals k divided by n is given by 'n choose k', multiplied by p raised to the power of k, and multiplied by the quantity one minus p, raised to the power of n minus k. This formula applies for k values ranging from zero up to n. Furthermore, the expected value of p-hat is equal to p. And the variance of p-hat is equal to p times the quantity one minus p, all divided by n. The standard error of p-hat, which represents the standard deviation of its sampling distribution, is equal to the square root of the quantity p times one minus p, all divided by n.

12.3 Exact sampling distributions closed under summation. Some families of distributions are stable under addition, making sample statistics particularly easy to analyze. For example, if a sequence of random variables, X sub one through X sub n, are independent and identically distributed, following a Poisson distribution with parameter lambda, then the sum of all X sub i from i equals one to n also follows a Poisson distribution with parameter n times lambda. And therefore, the sample mean, X bar sub n, is equal to one over n times a Poisson distribution with parameter n lambda. This is understood in the sense that n times X bar sub n actually has the Poisson law we just described. Consequently, the expected value of X bar sub n is equal to lambda, and the variance of X bar sub n is equal to lambda divided by n. Now, consider a different scenario. If a sequence of random variables, X sub one through X sub n, are independent and identically distributed, following a normal distribution with mean mu and variance sigma squared, then, exactly, for every value of n, the sample mean, X bar sub n, also follows a normal distribution with mean mu and variance sigma squared divided by n. No central limit approximation is needed in this case.

12.4 Standard deviation, standard error, and estimated standard error. The standard deviation of a population variable is a property of its population law. The standard error of a statistic, on the other hand, is the standard deviation of that statistic's sampling distribution. For the sample mean, which we denote as X-bar sub n, its standard error is equal to the square root of the variance of X-bar sub n. This is also equivalent to the population standard deviation, sigma, divided by the square root of n. If the population standard deviation, sigma, is unknown, we estimate this standard error. The estimated standard error of X-bar sub n, often indicated by a hat symbol over the standard error notation, is calculated as the sample standard deviation, S sub n, divided by the square root of n. These three quantities should not be confused: first, the population standard deviation, sigma; second, sigma divided by the square root of n; and third, the sample standard deviation, S sub n, divided by the square root of n. Remark twelve point two: Why standard errors shrink at the square-root rate. Averaging n independent observations divides the variance by n. Specifically, the variance of the sample mean, X-bar sub n, is equal to sigma squared, divided by n. Taking square roots, therefore, divides the uncertainty by the square root of n. To cut a standard error in half, one generally needs roughly four times as many independent observations.
