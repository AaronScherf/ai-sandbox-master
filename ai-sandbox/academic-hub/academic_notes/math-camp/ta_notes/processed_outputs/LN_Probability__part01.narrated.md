Introduction. Probability theory enters economics both as a language for uncertainty and as a theory of approximation. At the computational level, we manipulate events, conditional probabilities, random variables, densities, expectations, and familiar distribution families. At the rigorous level, the same objects are probability measures, measurable maps, integrals, conditional expectations relative to information, and modes of convergence. The aim of this part is to develop both levels together. The organizing progression is: first, probability spaces, leading to random variables, then expectation and conditioning, followed by sampling, then stochastic convergence, and finally, L L N and C L T. The elementary and rigorous treatments should reinforce one another. For instance, the expectation of X equals the sum over all possible values of x, of x multiplied by the probability that the random variable X takes on that value x. And, the expectation of X also equals the integral over the real numbers, of x multiplied by the probability density function of X at x, with respect to x. These are both special cases of the expectation of X equaling the integral over the sample space Omega, of the random variable X evaluated at an outcome omega, with respect to the probability measure P, at omega. Likewise, a conditional mean such as the expectation of Y given that the random variable X equals a specific value x, is the computational version of the conditional-expectation object, the expectation of Y conditioned on the sigma-algebra generated by X. And the statement that sample averages "settle down" separates into several distinct notions of stochastic convergence. A diagram, titled Figure 1, provides a roadmap for Part 4. 1.1 Outcomes, sample spaces, and events. A probability model begins with a set, which we denote by the capital Greek letter Omega, representing all possible outcomes. An event is a collection of these outcomes whose probability we wish to discuss. Let's consider Example 1.1: Two coin tosses. In this example, we define capital Omega as the set containing four specific outcomes: H H, H T, T H, and T T. The event "exactly one head" is defined as capital A, which is the set containing H T and T H. The event "the first toss is a head" is defined as capital B, which is the set containing H H and H T. Then, the intersection of A and B equals the set containing just H T. The union of A and B equals the set containing H H, H T, and T H. And the complement of B equals the set containing T H and T T. For models with a finite number of outcomes, one could assign probabilities to every single subset of capital Omega. However, on an uncountable sample space, that's generally too much to ask. We therefore specify which subsets count as measurable events.

1.2 Event algebra and basic probability identities. Before introducing sigma-algebras, it's useful to be completely fluent with the algebra of events. If we consider two events, A and B, as subsets of our sample space, Omega, then: The complement of event A is defined as all outcomes in the sample space Omega that are not in A. And the event "A minus B" – meaning outcomes in A but not in B – is equivalent to the intersection of A and the complement of B. The union of A and B means that at least one of A or B occurs. The intersection of A and B means that both A and B occur. De Morgan's laws state: First, the complement of the union of A and B is equal to the intersection of the complement of A and the complement of B. Second, the complement of the intersection of A and B is equal to the union of the complement of A and the complement of B. For a sequence of events, these laws extend to: The complement of the union of an infinite sequence of events, A sub n, is equal to the intersection of the complements of those events. And the complement of the intersection of an infinite sequence of events, A sub n, is equal to the union of the complements of those events. The most useful finite probability identities include: The probability of the complement of A is equal to one minus the probability of A. The probability of the union of A and B is equal to the probability of A plus the probability of B, minus the probability of the intersection of A and B. And, if A and B are disjoint events – meaning their intersection is the empty set – then: The probability of the union of A and B is simply the probability of A plus the probability of B. Let's consider Example 1.2: Translating words into events. Let A denote the event that "a household is liquidity constrained," and B denote the event that "the household is unemployed." Then: The event A union B means that at least one condition holds. The event A union B also means both conditions hold. The event A intersect B means constrained but employed. And, The expression, the complement of the intersection of A and B, which is equal to the union of the complement of A and the complement of B, means that at least one of the two conditions fails. A surprisingly large fraction of elementary probability mistakes occur before any probability is calculated: the event itself has been translated incorrectly.
