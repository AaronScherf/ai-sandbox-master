8.3 Strong law. Here is the passage rewritten for audio narration: Theorem 8.2 — Kolmogorov Strong Law for independent and identically distributed variables If the sequence X sub one, X sub two, and so on, are independent and identically distributed, and the expected value of the absolute value of X sub one is less than infinity, then X bar sub n converges almost surely to the expected value of X sub one. The full proof requires more machinery than the weak law. Its importance is conceptual: with probability one, a realized infinite sample path has sample averages converging to the population mean. Remark 8.3 — Rigorous layer: A Borel-Cantelli proof under stronger moments Suppose X sub i are independent and identically distributed with the expected value of X sub i equal to zero, and the expected value of X sub i to the power of four is less than infinity. One can show that the expected value of S sub n to the power of four is Big O of n squared, where S sub n is the sum from i equals one to n of X sub i. Hence, the probability that the absolute value of S sub n divided by n is greater than epsilon, is less than or equal to the expected value of S sub n to the power of four, divided by n to the power of four times epsilon to the power of four, which equals Big O of n to the power of minus two. The probabilities are summable, so Borel-Cantelli implies that S sub n divided by n converges to zero almost surely. This is not the sharpest strong law, but it reveals the logic: a summable tail bound upgrades probabilistic concentration to an almost-sure statement.

8.4 Exponential concentration: Chernoff and Hoeffding bounds. Chebyshev's inequality uses only a second moment and therefore gives polynomial tail bounds. If moment-generating functions are controlled, exponentially small tail probabilities are often available. For any t greater than zero, Markov's inequality gives: The probability that S sub n is greater than or equal to a , equals the probability that e to the power of t times S sub n is greater than or equal to e to the power of t times a . This is less than or equal to e to the power of negative t times a , multiplied by the expected value of e to the power of t times S sub n . If X sub one through X sub n are independent, the expected value of e to the power of t times S sub n equals the product from i equals one to n , of the expected value of e to the power of t times X sub i . Optimizing over t yields a Chernoff bound. Theorem 8.4 — Hoeffding inequality, bounded independent and identically distributed case If X sub one through X sub n are independent, with a less than or equal to X sub i , which is less than or equal to b , almost surely, and the expected value of X sub i equals mu sub i , then for X bar sub n , which equals one over n times the sum over i of X sub i , and mu bar sub n , which equals one over n times the sum over i of mu sub i : The probability that the absolute value of X bar sub n minus mu bar sub n is greater than or equal to epsilon , is less than or equal to two times the exponential of negative, two times n times epsilon squared, divided by the quantity b minus a , all squared. Unlike Chebyshev's big O of one over n bound for a fixed epsilon , Hoeffding gives exponential decay in n . Concentration inequalities are finite-sample counterparts to laws of large numbers and are increasingly important in modern statistics and econometrics.

8.5 Sample moments. If the expected value of the absolute value of X raised to the power of k is finite, the Law of Large Numbers tells us that the sample average of X sub i raised to the power of k, which is expressed as one over n times the sum from i equals one to n of X sub i to the k, converges in probability to the expected value of X raised to the power of k. Consequently, the sample mean squared deviation, calculated as one over n times the sum from i equals one to n of the quantity X sub i minus the sample mean X bar sub n, all squared, converges in probability to the variance of X, provided that the second moments are finite. More generally, sample moment conditions, given by one over n times the sum from i equals one to n of a function g, evaluated at W sub i and theta, converge to their corresponding population moments, which are the expected value of the function g, evaluated at W and theta. This convergence occurs under appropriate conditions for the Law of Large Numbers. This principle forms the probabilistic foundation for estimation methods such as the method of moments and Generalized Method of Moments estimation.

8.6 Empirical distribution and Glivenko-Cantelli. Given a set of independent and identically distributed observations, X sub one through X sub n, we define the empirical cumulative distribution function as follows: F sub n of x is defined as the average, over all i from one to n, of an indicator variable. This indicator variable is one if X sub i is less than or equal to x, and zero otherwise. For each fixed value x, the Law of Large Numbers tells us that F sub n of x converges in probability to F of x. A much stronger result is given by the Glivenko-Cantelli theorem: The supremum, over all x in the set of real numbers, of the absolute value of the difference between F sub n of x and F of x, converges almost surely to zero. This means that the empirical distribution converges uniformly to the population distribution. The Dvoretzky-Kiefer-Wolfowitz inequality provides a finite-sample bound: The probability that the supremum over all x of the absolute value of the difference between F sub n of x and F of x is greater than epsilon, is less than or equal to two times e, raised to the power of negative two n epsilon squared. This is considered an early example of a uniform law of large numbers.

8.7 Beyond iid: what changes?. Independence is sufficient, not necessary. Many economic data are dependent over time or within groups. Laws of large numbers continue to hold under weak dependence, mixing, martingale-difference, ergodic, or cluster structures, but the required conditions differ. The core lesson is that one needs a mechanism preventing dependence from accumulating too quickly.

9.1 Why the \sqrt{n} scaling appears. Here's the passage rewritten for audio narration: Under independent and identically distributed sampling, where the population has a variance we denote as sigma squared, the variance of the sample mean, often written as X-bar sub n, is equal to sigma squared divided by the sample size n. This means that if we take the square root of n, and multiply it by the difference between the sample mean X-bar sub n and the population mean mu, the resulting quantity has a variance equal to sigma squared. Crucially, this variance does not vanish; it doesn't go to zero. The central limit theorem then identifies its limiting distribution.

9.2 Lindeberg-Lévy CLT. Here is Theorem nine point one, known as the Classical Central Limit Theorem. It states that if X sub one, X sub two, and so on, are independent and identically distributed random variables, with their expected value of X sub i equal to mu, and their variance of X sub i, denoted as sigma squared, being finite and positive, then the expression: the square root of n, multiplied by the quantity X bar sub n minus mu, all divided by sigma, converges in distribution to a normal distribution with mean zero and variance one. Equivalently, the expression: the square root of n, multiplied by the quantity X bar sub n minus mu, converges in distribution to a normal distribution with mean zero and variance sigma squared. The Law of Large Numbers says the error vanishes. The Central Limit Theorem says the error is typically of order n to the minus one half, and describes its shape after rescaling.

9.3 Characteristic-function proof. Let's define a variable, Y sub i, as the quantity X sub i minus mu, divided by sigma. For this variable, the expected value of Y sub i is zero, and the expected value of Y sub i squared is one. For small values of t, the characteristic function of Y, denoted phi sub Y of t, is equal to one minus t squared over two, plus little-o of t squared. Now, consider a new variable, Z sub n, defined as one over the square root of n, multiplied by the sum of Y sub i from i equals one to n. Due to the independence of the Y sub i variables, the characteristic function of Z sub n at t is equal to the characteristic function of Y, evaluated at t divided by the square root of n, and then that entire quantity is raised to the power of n. Applying the local expansion for phi sub Y, evaluated at t divided by the square root of n, we get one minus t squared divided by two n, plus little-o of n to the power of negative one. Therefore, as n approaches infinity, the characteristic function of Z sub n at t converges to the exponential of negative t squared over two. This is the characteristic function of a standard normal distribution, denoted N of zero, one. Lévy's continuity theorem then tells us that Z sub n converges in distribution to a standard normal distribution, N of zero, one. Now, a remark on the rigorous layer. Remark 9.2: What Lévy's continuity theorem contributes. Characteristic functions uniquely determine probability laws. Lévy's continuity theorem strengthens this uniqueness into a powerful convergence statement. It says that if characteristic functions converge pointwise to a function that is continuous at zero and is itself a characteristic function, then this implies the weak convergence of the associated probability measures.

9.4 Multivariate CLT. Let X sub i be a k-dimensional real vector, independent and identically distributed, with expected value E of X sub i equal to mu, and covariance matrix Var of X sub i equal to Sigma, where Sigma is finite. Then, the expression: the square root of n multiplied by the difference between X bar sub n and mu, converges in distribution to a k-dimensional normal distribution with mean vector zero and covariance matrix Sigma. Proof For any k-dimensional real vector a, the expression: a transpose multiplied by the square root of n times the difference between X bar sub n and mu, is equal to the square root of n multiplied by the quantity: one over n times the sum from i equals one to n of a transpose X sub i, minus a transpose mu. The scalar Central Limit Theorem then states that the expression: a transpose multiplied by the square root of n times the difference between X bar sub n and mu, converges in distribution to a normal distribution with mean zero and variance a transpose Sigma a. By the Cramer-Wold theorem, the vector converges to a k-dimensional normal distribution with mean vector zero and covariance matrix Sigma.

And suppose that the function g is differentiable at the point theta. Here, the ratio of r sub n to the quantity T sub n minus theta converges in probability to zero. Since T sub n converges in probability to theta, we can write the square root of n times r sub n as the square root of n times the quantity T sub n minus theta, multiplied by the ratio of r sub n to the quantity T sub n minus theta. This entire expression converges in probability to zero. Therefore, the square root of n times the quantity g of T sub n minus g of theta equals g prime of theta multiplied by the square root of n times the quantity T sub n minus theta, plus a term that is little o p of one. 9.6 Asymptotic linearization. Many estimators can be described by an expansion. This expansion states that the square root of n, multiplied by the quantity theta-hat sub n minus theta sub zero, equals one over the square root of n, multiplied by the sum from i equals one to n of psi of W sub i, plus little o sub p of one. Here, the expected value of psi of W sub i equals zero. If the variance of psi of W sub i equals Omega, and Omega is less than infinity, then the Central Limit Theorem and Slutsky's Theorem imply that the square root of n, multiplied by the quantity theta-hat sub n minus theta sub zero, converges in distribution to a normal distribution with mean zero and variance Omega. This representation is the generic architecture behind smooth method-of-moments estimators, maximum likelihood estimators, and influence-function methods. The difficult part of a new econometric problem is often to identify the correct first-order term, psi, and show that the remainder is little o sub p of one.

9.7 Studentization and plug-in variance estimation. Suppose that the product of the square root of n and the quantity T sub n minus theta converges in distribution to a normal distribution with a mean of zero and a variance of V. And V-hat sub n converges in probability to V, where V is greater than zero. Then, the ratio of the square root of n times the quantity T sub n minus theta, to the square root of V-hat sub n, converges in distribution to a standard normal distribution, by Slutsky's theorem. This is the generic logic behind asymptotic t-statistics.

9.9 Berry-Esseen and rates. The Central Limit Theorem is qualitative. If the third absolute moment is finite, the Berry-Esseen theorem provides a uniform error bound of the order of n to the power of negative one-half. Specifically, the maximum value, over all possible x , of the absolute difference between two quantities. The first quantity is the probability that the expression 'square root of n times the difference between the sample mean X-bar-n and the population mean mu , all divided by the population standard deviation sigma ' is less than or equal to x . The second quantity is the value of the standard normal cumulative distribution function at x . This absolute difference is less than or equal to a fraction. The numerator of this fraction is the universal constant C multiplied by the expected value of the absolute difference between a random variable X and its mean mu , raised to the power of three. The denominator is the population standard deviation sigma cubed, multiplied by the square root of n . This clarifies why a Central Limit Theorem can be asymptotically correct, meaning accurate in very large samples, but still inaccurate in small samples, especially when the distribution's tails are heavy or its skewness is large. Remark nine point four: A more rigorous layer, involving triangular arrays and the Lindeberg-Feller theorem. In many statistical estimators, the individual terms being summed depend on n , the sample size. A Central Limit Theorem for triangular arrays considers a sequence of independent variables, each with a mean of zero. These variables are denoted as X-n-one , up to X-n-k-n , where k-n is the number of variables in the n -th row of the array. The total variance for this array, s-n squared, is the sum, from j equals one to k-n , of the variance of each variable X-n-j . A Lindeberg condition, for example, states that the expression 'one over s-n squared, multiplied by the sum over j of the expected value of X-n-j squared, multiplied by an indicator function that takes the value one when the absolute value of X-n-j is greater than epsilon times s-n , and zero otherwise'—this entire expression approaches zero for all epsilon greater than zero. This condition prevents any single large term from disproportionately influencing the sum. Under appropriate normalization of the variance, the sum over j of X-n-j , divided by s-n , converges in distribution to a standard normal distribution, that is, a normal distribution with a mean of zero and a variance of one. This framework provides a more useful template for modern asymptotic theory than relying solely on the independent and identically distributed Central Limit Theorem.
