12.5 Normal samples: the exact z, \chi^2, t , and F structure. Suppose our random variables, X sub one through X sub n, are independently and identically distributed as a normal distribution with mean mu and variance sigma squared. Then, the variable Z, defined as the square root of n multiplied by the quantity X bar sub n minus mu, all divided by sigma, follows a standard normal distribution. And the variable Q, defined as the quantity n minus one multiplied by S sub n squared, all divided by sigma squared, is distributed as a chi-squared distribution with n minus one degrees of freedom. And X bar sub n is independent of S sub n squared. Consequently, the variable T, defined as the square root of n multiplied by the quantity X bar sub n minus mu, all divided by S sub n, which is equivalent to Z divided by the square root of the quantity Q divided by n minus one, follows a t-distribution with n minus one degrees of freedom. The distinction between Z and T is operationally important: If sigma is known, we use Z. Whereas if sigma is unknown and replaced by S sub n, we use T. For two independent normal samples, the ratio of normalized sample variances yields the F distribution described in the previous section.

12.6 Simple random sampling without replacement. The I.I.D. framework, standing for independent and identically distributed, corresponds naturally to sampling with replacement from an idealized population law. Finite-population sampling, however, introduces a useful correction when observations are drawn without replacement. Let's consider a set of fixed population values: y sub one, y sub two, and so on, up to y sub N. For this population, we define the finite-population mean and variance as follows: The mean, mu sub N, is equal to one over N times the sum from i equals one to N of y sub i. And the variance, S sub N squared, is equal to one over N minus one times the sum from i equals one to N of the quantity y sub i minus mu sub N, all squared. Now, imagine we draw a simple random sample of size n without replacement, and let Y bar sub s be the sample mean. Then, the expected value of Y bar sub s is equal to mu sub N. And the variance of Y bar sub s is given by S sub N squared divided by n, multiplied by the quantity one minus the fraction n over N. The factor, one minus the fraction n over N, is known as the finite-population correction. When this sampling fraction, n over N, is negligible, the I.I.D. variance formula provides an excellent approximation. However, when a large fraction of the population is observed, the sampling uncertainty becomes smaller because observations are negatively dependent under sampling without replacement.

12.7 Normal approximation to the binomial and continuity correction. For a random variable X that follows a binomial distribution with parameters n and p, provided that the values of n times p and n times the quantity one minus p are not too small, the standardized variable—that is, the quantity X minus n times p, divided by the square root of n times p times the quantity one minus p—is approximately standard normal. Thus, X is approximately distributed as a normal distribution with a mean of n times p, and a variance of n times p times the quantity one minus p. Because the binomial is discrete and the normal is continuous, a continuity correction often improves the approximation. For example, the probability that X is less than or equal to k is approximately equal to Phi—the capital Greek letter Phi, representing the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution—evaluated at the value obtained by taking k plus one-half minus n times p, and dividing that by the square root of n times p times the quantity one minus p. Similarly, the probability that X is greater than or equal to k is approximately equal to one minus Phi, evaluated at the value obtained by taking k minus one-half minus n times p, and dividing that by the square root of n times p times the quantity one minus p. Example 12.3 — A binomial probability by normal approximation Suppose X is distributed as a binomial distribution with n equal to one hundred and p equal to zero point four. Then, the expected value of X is forty, and the standard deviation of X is the square root of twenty-four. To approximate the probability that X is less than or equal to forty-five, we use the following: The probability that X is less than or equal to forty-five is approximately equal to Phi, evaluated at the value obtained by taking forty-five point five minus forty, and dividing that by the square root of twenty-four. The zero point five adjustment matches the discrete cutoff at forty-five to the corresponding interval under the continuous approximation.

12.8 When to use an exact distribution and when to use an asymptotic one. Here's a useful hierarchy to guide your approach: First, if an exact distribution for a finite sample is available, then you should use it. Otherwise, if an exact distribution is not available, then you should look for a useful approximation. Common examples of such approximations include the Poisson distribution, the normal distribution, or the Central Limit Theorem. For instance, consider these examples: * When you sum up several independent Bernoulli random variables, the result follows an exact binomial distribution. * If you have a collection of independent counts, each following a Poisson distribution, their sum will also follow an exact Poisson distribution. * The mean of a sample drawn from a Gaussian, or normal, distribution will itself be exactly Gaussian. * For sample means from a more general distribution, they will be approximately Gaussian when the sample size, which we denote as 'n', is large, provided the conditions for the Central Limit Theorem are met. It's important to note that the word 'approximately' carries significant mathematical meaning here. Asymptotic theory, a branch of mathematics, describes an approximation whose quality typically improves as the sample size increases. However, this approximation doesn't necessarily have to be exact for any specific, finite sample size.

13 Hazards, Poisson Arrivals, and Simulation. Sources. The first half of this section develops the probability tools behind duration models, search models, default risk, and arrival processes. The second half uses the probability integral transform together with the LLN and CLT to explain simulation and Monte Carlo approximation.

13.1 Survival functions and hazard rates. Let T be a duration that is continuously distributed, meaning it's a non-negative value, with a cumulative distribution function, F, and a probability density function, f. The survival function, denoted S of t, is defined as the probability that T is greater than t. This is equal to one minus the cumulative distribution function F of t. The hazard rate, denoted h of t, is the limit as Delta approaches zero from above, of the probability that T is between t and t plus Delta, given that T is greater than or equal to t, all divided by Delta. This can also be expressed as the probability density function f of t, divided by the survival function S of t, whenever S of t is greater than zero. We define the cumulative hazard, H of t, as the integral from zero to t of h of s, with respect to s. Since the derivative of S of t with respect to t is equal to negative f of t, which is also equal to negative h of t times S of t, we obtain that S of t equals e raised to the power of negative H of t. Also, F of t equals one minus e raised to the power of negative H of t. And f of t equals h of t times e raised to the power of negative H of t. Thus, a hazard function completely determines the entire duration distribution. Example 13.1: Exponential duration and constant hazard If T follows an exponential distribution with parameter lambda, then S of t equals e raised to the power of negative lambda t. The hazard rate h of t equals lambda. And the cumulative hazard H of t equals lambda t. This constant hazard is equivalent to the property of memorylessness. Weibull benchmark. A flexible one-parameter departure from constant hazard uses a survival function S of t equal to the exponential of negative, open parenthesis, t divided by lambda, close parenthesis, all raised to the power of k. This applies for t greater than or equal to zero. Which implies that the hazard rate h of t equals k divided by lambda, multiplied by open parenthesis, t divided by lambda, close parenthesis, all raised to the power of k minus one. Hence, if k is greater than one, this gives an increasing hazard. If k equals one, this gives the exponential case. And if k is less than one, this gives a decreasing hazard.

13.2 Competing exponential clocks. Suppose that T sub j, for j from one to capital J, are independent waiting times for different events, where each T sub j follows an exponential distribution with rate parameter lambda sub j. Let T be the minimum of all these waiting times, T sub j. Then, the probability that T is greater than t equals the product, from j equals one to capital J, of the probability that T sub j is greater than t. This is equal to the exponential of negative t multiplied by the sum, from j equals one to capital J, of lambda sub j. Therefore, T itself follows an exponential distribution with a rate parameter equal to the sum of all the individual lambda sub j's. Moreover, the probability that a specific waiting time, T sub j, is the minimum waiting time T, is given by lambda sub j divided by the sum, from L equals one to capital J, of all the lambda sub L's. This means that independent hazards simply add up, and the probability that event j occurs first is equal to its proportion of the total hazard. Let's consider Example 13.2, illustrating job finding versus exit from the labor force. Suppose an unemployed worker receives job offers at a Poisson rate of lambda, and exits the labor force at a rate of delta, with these two processes governed by independent exponential clocks. The total time until the unemployment spell ends, which we'll call T, follows an exponential distribution with a rate parameter equal to lambda plus delta. Therefore, the expected value of T, or the average time until the spell ends, is one divided by the sum of lambda and delta. Conditional on the unemployment spell ending, the probability that it ends in employment is lambda divided by the sum of lambda and delta. And the probability of labor-force exit is delta divided by the sum of lambda and delta. This represents the basic probability calculation underlying continuous-time competing-risks models.

13.3 The Poisson process. A counting process, denoted N of t, for time t greater than or equal to zero, is defined as a homogeneous Poisson process with a rate parameter, lambda, which is strictly greater than zero, if the following conditions are met: First, the count at time zero, N of zero, is equal to zero. Second, the process has independent increments. And third, for any two time points, s and t, where s is less than t and both are non-negative, the number of events occurring between time s and time t, which is N of t minus N of s, follows a Poisson distribution with a mean parameter given by lambda multiplied by the duration t minus s. More specifically, the count at time t, N of t, follows a Poisson distribution with a mean parameter of lambda times t. Consequently, its expected value, denoted E of N of t, and its variance, denoted Var of N of t, are both equal to lambda times t. Now, let T sub one be the random variable representing the time of the first arrival. Then, the probability that the first arrival time, T sub one, is greater than some time t, is equal to the probability that the count at time t, N of t, is zero. This probability is given by the mathematical constant e, raised to the power of negative lambda times t. Therefore, T sub one follows an exponential distribution with a rate parameter of lambda. The independent-increments property of the Poisson process implies that successive interarrival times are independent and identically distributed exponential random variables. Consequently, the time of the r-th arrival, denoted T sub r, is the sum of r independent and identically distributed random variables, W sub one through W sub r. Each W sub j represents an interarrival time and follows an exponential distribution with rate parameter lambda. This sum, T sub r, therefore has a gamma distribution. Specifically, T sub r follows a gamma distribution with shape parameter r and rate parameter lambda. This elegantly unifies the Poisson, exponential, and gamma families of distributions through a single arrival-process model.

13.4 Superposition and thinning of Poisson processes. Imagine we have two independent Poisson processes. Let's call the first one N sub one at time t, and the second one N sub two at time t. These processes have their own rates: lambda sub one for the first, and lambda sub two for the second. If these conditions hold, then a new process, N at time t, formed by adding N sub one at time t and N sub two at time t, will also be a Poisson process. And its rate will be the sum of the individual rates: lambda sub one plus lambda sub two. This property is the process-level equivalent of the well-known fact that independent Poisson random variables also add together. Now, let's consider the reverse scenario. Suppose we start with a single Poisson process, which has a rate we'll call lambda. Imagine that each arrival in this process is independently sorted or classified. For example, it might be classified as type one with a probability of p, and as type two with a probability of one minus p. In this case, the separate counting processes for each type—type one and type two—will themselves be independent Poisson processes. The rate for the type one process will be p times lambda, and the rate for the type two process will be one minus p times lambda. This particular characteristic, known as the "thinning property," proves very useful whenever a single, common stream of arrivals is randomly split or divided into different destinations, types, or outcomes.

13.5 Probability integral transform. Here's Proposition thirteen point three, also known as the Probability Integral Transform. If a random variable, which we'll call X, has a continuous cumulative distribution function, denoted as F, then a new random variable, U, defined as F of X, is uniformly distributed between zero and one. Conversely, if U is uniformly distributed between zero and one, and F inverse is the generalized inverse cumulative distribution function, then the random variable X, defined as F inverse of U, has a cumulative distribution function equal to F. For the forward direction, if a value u is greater than zero and less than one, and F is strictly increasing, then the probability that F of X is less than or equal to u, is equal to the probability that X is less than or equal to F inverse of u. And this value is simply u. The generalized inverse formulation allows this result to be extended beyond cases where F is strictly increasing.

13.6 Inverse-transform simulation. Here's the passage rewritten for natural spoken prose, describing mathematical notation in words: The probability integral transform provides a general recipe for simulation. First, U is drawn from a uniform distribution between zero and one. Then, X is calculated as F inverse of U. Let's look at an example. Example thirteen point four: Simulating an exponential random variable. For X, which follows an exponential distribution with parameter lambda, the cumulative distribution function F of x is equal to one minus e to the power of negative lambda x, for x greater than or equal to zero. We set U equal to F of X and then solve for X. This means U equals one minus e to the power of negative lambda X. And solving for X gives us X equals negative one over lambda, multiplied by the natural logarithm of the quantity one minus U. Since one minus U also follows a uniform distribution between zero and one, the expression is usually simplified to: X equals negative one over lambda, multiplied by the natural logarithm of U.

13.7 Monte Carlo integration. Suppose the target quantity, which we'll call theta, can be written as the expected value of a function g of a random variable X. If we can simulate independent and identically distributed draws, X sub one through X sub M, from some distribution F, then the Monte Carlo estimator, theta hat sub M, is given by one over M times the sum, from m equals one to M, of g of X sub m. If the expected value of the absolute value of g of X is finite, then the Law of Large Numbers tells us that theta hat sub M converges almost surely to theta. If additionally the variance of g of X, which we'll call tau squared, is finite, then the Central Limit Theorem tells us that the square root of M times the quantity theta hat sub M minus theta converges in distribution to a normal distribution with mean zero and variance tau squared. Therefore, the Monte Carlo standard error is approximately tau divided by the square root of M, and can be estimated by s-e hat sub M C, which equals s sub g divided by the square root of M, where s sub g squared is the sample variance of the simulated values g of X sub m. Remark thirteen point five: Simulation error versus sampling error Monte Carlo error arises from using a finite number of simulated draws to approximate a mathematical expectation. Sampling error comes from observing a finite amount of real data. They are conceptually different. In a computational exercise, both types of error may be present, and increasing the number of simulations reduces Monte Carlo error without creating additional empirical information.

13.8 Importance sampling: a change-of-measure identity. Sometimes, when drawing a sample X from a probability distribution P, it can be difficult to simulate efficiently. Alternatively, the important region of the state space might be rare under that distribution P. If P has a probability density function, which we'll call lowercase p, and another distribution Q has its own probability density function, which we'll call lowercase q, and these densities satisfy a certain condition: that whenever the density p at x is greater than zero, it implies that the density q at x is also greater than zero. Then the following identity holds: The expected value of a function g of X, calculated under the distribution P, is equal to the integral over all possible values of x, of the function g of x multiplied by the density p of x. This is also equal to the integral over all x of g of x, multiplied by the fraction p of x over q of x, and then multiplied by q of x, all integrated with respect to x. And this, in turn, is equal to the expected value, calculated under distribution Q, of the product of g of X and the ratio of p of X to q of X. Thus, one may simulate samples from distribution Q and then reweight them using a factor called the likelihood ratio. This likelihood ratio, denoted w of x, is defined as the ratio of the density p of x to the density q of x. This is the basic probability identity that underpins importance sampling and many arguments involving likelihood ratios.
