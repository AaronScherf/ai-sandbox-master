Introduction Probability theory finds its way into economics both as a language for describing uncertainty and as a framework for approximation. At a computational level, we work with events, conditional probabilities, random variables, densities, expectations, and well-known distribution families. At a more rigorous level, these same concepts are understood as probability measures, measurable maps, integrals, conditional expectations relative to information, and various modes of convergence. The goal of this section is to develop both these computational and rigorous levels in tandem. The organizing progression of topics is as follows: we start with probability spaces, which then lead to random variables, followed by expectation and conditioning, then sampling, which progresses to stochastic convergence, and finally to the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem. The elementary and rigorous treatments are designed to reinforce one another. For example, the expected value of X, written as E of X, equals the sum over all possible values of x of x multiplied by the probability that the random variable X equals x. And, alternatively, the expected value of X, or E of X, equals the integral over the real numbers of x multiplied by the probability density function of X at x, with respect to x. Both of these expressions are special cases of... The expected value of X is equal to the integral over the sample space Omega of X of omega, with respect to the probability measure P of omega. Likewise, a conditional mean, such as the expected value of Y given that X equals x, is the computational version of the conditional-expectation object, which is the expected value of Y given the sigma-algebra generated by X. And the statement that sample averages “settle down” separates into several distinct notions of stochastic convergence. An event, then, is simply a collection of these outcomes, for which we want to determine a probability. Example 1.1 — Two coin tosses Let's consider Example 1.1, involving two coin tosses. In this scenario, our set Omega, representing all possible outcomes, would be the set containing Head-Head, Head-Tail, Tail-Head, and Tail-Tail. The event we call A, which describes 'exactly one head,' would then be the set containing Head-Tail and Tail-Head. Now, let's define event B as 'the first toss is a head.' This means B is the set containing Head-Head and Head-Tail. From these definitions, we can derive a few things. The intersection of A and B, written as A intersect B, is the set containing just Head-Tail. The union of A and B, written as A union B, is the set containing Head-Head, Head-Tail, and Tail-Head. And the complement of B, written as B superscript c, is the set containing Tail-Head and Tail-Tail. For probability models with a finite number of outcomes, it's possible to assign probabilities to every single subset of Omega. However, when dealing with an uncountable sample space, that's generally too complex a task. Because of this, we need to specifically define which subsets are considered 'measurable events' for our analysis. 1.2 Event algebra and basic probability identities Before we dive into sigma-algebras, it's really helpful to be completely comfortable with the basic algebra of events. So, if A and B are any subsets of Omega, then the complement of A, written as A superscript c, is equal to Omega minus A. And the set A minus B is equivalent to the intersection of A and the complement of B, or A intersect B superscript c. The event A union B means that 'at least one of A or B occurs.' And the event A intersect B means that 'both A and B occur.' De Morgan's laws provide important relationships for complements. The first law states that the complement of A union B is equal to the intersection of A complement and B complement. The second law states that the complement of A intersect B is equal to the union of A complement and B complement. These laws also extend to sequences of events. For an infinite sequence, the complement of the infinite union of events A sub n is equal to the infinite intersection of the complements of A sub n. And conversely, the complement of the infinite intersection of events A sub n is equal to the infinite union of the complements of A sub n. Now, let's look at some of the most useful probability identities for finite cases. First, the probability of A complement is equal to one minus the probability of A. Second, the probability of A union B is equal to the probability of A plus the probability of B, minus the probability of A intersect B. And finally, if A and B are disjoint events, meaning their intersection is the empty set, then the probability of A union B is simply equal to the probability of A plus the probability of B. Omega itself is an element of script F. 2. If any set A is an element of script F, then its complement, A complement, must also be an element of script F. 3. If A-sub-1, A-sub-2, and so on, are all elements of script F, then the union of A-sub-n for n from 1 to infinity must also be an element of script F. The pair consisting of Omega and script F is called a measurable space. Closure under complements and countable unions implies closure under countable intersections by De Morgan’s law: The intersection of A-sub-n for n from 1 to infinity is equal to the complement of the union of the complements of A-sub-n, also for n from 1 to infinity. The insistence on countable operations is essential. Limits of sequences are central in probability, and events such as the event where X-sub-n converges to X are built from countable unions and intersections. Example 1.5 — Sigma-algebra generated by a partition Suppose Omega is the set containing the numbers 1, 2, 3, and 4. And the only distinction an observer can make is whether the outcome lies in the set containing 1 and 2, or the set containing 3 and 4. Then the natural information set, script F, is the set containing the empty set, the set containing 1 and 2, the set containing 3 and 4, and Omega itself. This is a sigma-algebra. It records exactly the events that can be decided from the observer’s information. 1.4 Generated sigma-algebras and Borel sets Given a family, denoted by script C, which is a collection of subsets of Omega, the sigma-algebra generated by script C is defined as: Sigma of script C equals the intersection of all collections script F, such that script F is a sigma-algebra and script C is a subset of script F. It is the smallest sigma-algebra containing script C. On the set of real numbers, denoted R, the most important sigma-algebra is the Borel sigma-algebra. This is denoted by script B of R, and is defined as the sigma-algebra generated by the collection of all intervals extending from negative infinity up to and including any real number x. Equivalently, it is generated by the open sets. For k-dimensional Euclidean space, denoted R to the power of k, we write script B of R to the k. Remark 1.6 — Rigorous layer: Why Borel sets are enough for most economics A random variable taking values in R to the k will be required to be measurable with respect to the Borel sigma-algebra on R to the k. If A sub 1, A sub 2, and so on, are a sequence of sets belonging to script F and are pairwise disjoint, then capital P of the union from n equals 1 to infinity of A sub n, equals the sum from n equals 1 to infinity of capital P of A sub n. The triple, consisting of Omega, script F, and capital P, is called a probability space. Immediate consequences include: capital P of the empty set equals 0; capital P of A complement equals 1 minus capital P of A; if A is a subset of B, then capital P of A is less than or equal to capital P of B; and capital P of A union B equals capital P of A plus capital P of B minus capital P of A intersection B. More generally, Boole’s inequality states that capital P of the union from n equals 1 to infinity of A sub n, is less than or equal to the sum from n equals 1 to infinity of capital P of A sub n. 1.6 Continuity of probability Proposition 1.8 — Continuity from below If we have an increasing sequence of sets, where A sub 1 is a subset of A sub 2, and so on, and A is defined as the union from n equals 1 to infinity of A sub n, then capital P of A sub n converges to capital P of A. This follows by applying continuity from below to the complements. 1.7 Conditional probability, total probability, and Bayes’ rule Conditioning changes the relevant population. If the probability of event B is greater than zero, then among the outcomes for which B occurs, the fraction for which A also occurs is the conditional probability of A given B, which is equal to the probability of the intersection of A and B, divided by the probability of B. Equivalently, the multiplication rule states that the probability of the intersection of A and B is equal to the conditional probability of A given B, multiplied by the probability of B. This is also equal to the conditional probability of B given A, multiplied by the probability of A. More generally, the probability of the intersection of events A sub one through A sub n is equal to the probability of A sub one, multiplied by a product from j equals two to n, of the conditional probability of A sub j, given the intersection of events A sub one through A sub j minus one. This holds whenever the conditioning events have positive probability. Example 1.10 — Cards without replacement Draw two cards without replacement from a standard 52-card deck. Let A be the event that the first card is an ace and B the event that the second card is an ace. Then, the probability of A is four divided by fifty-two. And the conditional probability of B given A is three divided by fifty-one. Hence, the probability of the intersection of A and B is equal to four divided by fifty-two, multiplied by three divided by fifty-one, which equals one divided by two hundred twenty-one. The events are not independent: observing an ace on the first draw changes the probability of an ace on the second draw. Suppose events B sub one through B sub m form a partition of the sample space Omega, with the probability of each B sub j being greater than zero. Then, event A can be expressed as the union of the event A intersected with B sub one, the event A intersected with B sub two, and so on, up to the event A intersected with B sub m. This is a disjoint union, so the law of total probability states that the probability of A is equal to the sum, from j equals one to m, of the conditional probability of A given B sub j, multiplied by the probability of B sub j. Combining this identity with the multiplication rule yields Bayes' formula: The conditional probability of B sub j given A is equal to the conditional probability of A given B sub j, multiplied by the probability of B sub j, all divided by the sum, from L equals one to m, of the conditional probability of A given B sub L, multiplied by the probability of B sub L. The probability of a positive test given the condition is not present, or D complement, is zero point zero five. And the probability of the condition being present, is zero point zero two. The unconditional positive-test probability is equal to zero point nine five multiplied by zero point zero two, plus zero point zero five multiplied by zero point nine eight, which equals zero point zero six eight. The rigorous hierarchy of almost sure, probability, L-P, and distributional convergence draws from the works of Klenke, Durrett, and Billingsley. Definition 7.1 — Almost-sure convergence We write that X sub n converges almost surely to X. This means that the probability of the set of all outcomes omega, such that X sub n of omega converges to X of omega, is equal to one. Definition 7.2 — Convergence in probability We write that X sub n converges in probability to X. This holds if, for every epsilon greater than zero, the probability that the absolute difference between X sub n and X is greater than epsilon, approaches zero. Definition 7.3 — L-P convergence For a value P greater than or equal to one, we say X sub n converges in L-P to X. This is true if the expected value of the absolute difference between X sub n and X, raised to the power of P, approaches zero. Definition 7.4 — Convergence in distribution We write that X sub n converges in distribution to X. This occurs if the cumulative distribution function of X sub n, evaluated at x, approaches the cumulative distribution function of X, evaluated at x, for every point x where the cumulative distribution function of X is continuous. The primary implication structure is as follows: if X sub n converges almost surely to X, this implies that X sub n converges in probability to X, which in turn implies that X sub n converges in distribution to X. Additionally, if X sub n converges in L-P to X, this implies that X sub n converges in probability to X. It's important to note that the reverse implications do not hold true in general. Proof. To demonstrate that L-P convergence implies convergence in probability, we can apply Markov's inequality. The probability that the absolute difference between X sub n and X is greater than epsilon, is equal to the probability that the absolute difference between X sub n and X, raised to the power of P, is greater than epsilon raised to the power of P. By Markov's inequality, this is less than or equal to the expected value of the absolute difference between X sub n and X, raised to the power of P, all divided by epsilon raised to the power of P. As n approaches infinity, this entire expression approaches zero. Proposition 7.5 — Almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability If X sub n converges almost surely to X, then X sub n converges in probability to X. Proof. Let's fix an epsilon greater than zero. We define the set A sub N as the union, for all n greater than or equal to N, of the events where the absolute difference between X sub n and X is greater than epsilon. As N increases, the sets A sub N are decreasing, converging to a set A. This set A is a subset of the event where X sub n does not converge to X. Because of almost sure convergence, the probability of set A is zero. By the property of continuity from above for probability measures, the probability of A sub N approaches zero as N goes to infinity. Given that the probability of the absolute difference between X sub N and X being greater than epsilon is less than or equal to the probability of A sub N, convergence in probability is thus established. Proposition 7.6 — Convergence in probability implies convergence in distribution If X sub n converges in probability to X, then X sub n converges in distribution to X. A convenient proof uses the following inequalities. For every epsilon greater than zero, The probability that X sub n is less than or equal to x, is less than or equal to the probability that X is less than or equal to x plus epsilon, plus the probability that the absolute value of the difference between X sub n and X is greater than epsilon. The word 'approximately' here carries significant mathematical meaning. Asymptotic theory describes an approximation whose accuracy generally gets better as the sample size increases, but it's important to remember that it doesn't have to be exact for any specific, finite sample size. 13 Hazards, Poisson Arrivals, and Simulation Sources. This distribution has a cumulative distribution function, which we'll call F, and a probability density function, which we'll call f. The 'survival function', denoted S of t, is defined as the probability that the duration T is greater than a specific time 't'. This is also equivalent to one minus the cumulative distribution function F evaluated at time 't'. The hazard rate, denoted as h of t, is defined as the limit, as a small time increment Delta approaches zero from above, of the probability that the event occurs between time t and t plus Delta, given that it has not occurred before time t, all divided by Delta. This is also equivalent to the probability density function f of t, divided by the survival function S of t, provided that S of t is greater than zero. We then define the cumulative hazard, H of t, as the integral of the hazard rate h of s, with respect to s, from zero to t. Since we know that the derivative of the survival function S of t, with respect to t, equals the negative of the probability density function f of t, which also equals the negative of the hazard rate h of t multiplied by the survival function S of t, we can derive the following relationships: The survival function S of t equals e raised to the power of negative H of t. The cumulative distribution function F of t equals one minus e raised to the power of negative H of t. And the probability density function f of t equals the hazard rate h of t multiplied by e raised to the power of negative H of t. Therefore, knowing the hazard function allows us to determine the entire duration distribution. Example 13.1: Exponential Duration and Constant Hazard If the random variable T, representing duration, follows an exponential distribution with rate parameter lambda, then we have: The survival function S of t equals e raised to the power of negative lambda times t. The hazard rate h of t is simply lambda. And the cumulative hazard H of t is lambda times t. This constant hazard rate is equivalent to the property of memorylessness. Weibull Benchmark. Each T sub j follows an exponential distribution with its own rate parameter, lambda sub j, for j ranging from one to J. Let T be defined as the minimum of all these T sub j waiting times. Then, the probability that T is greater than t equals the product, from j equals one to J, of the probabilities that each individual T sub j is greater than t. This product simplifies to the exponential of negative t, multiplied by the sum of all lambda sub j, from j equals one to J. This means that T itself follows an exponential distribution with a rate parameter equal to the sum of all individual lambda sub j, from j equals one to J. Furthermore, the probability that a specific event j, represented by T sub j, is the first to occur, meaning T sub j equals T, is given by lambda sub j divided by the sum of all lambda sub L, for L ranging from one to J. Therefore, independent hazards simply add up, and the probability that a specific event j occurs first is equal to its individual hazard rate's share of the total combined hazard rate. Example 13.2: Job Finding Versus Exit from the Labor Force Imagine an unemployed worker who receives job offers at a Poisson rate of lambda, and exits the labor force at a rate of delta. These two processes are governed by independent exponential clocks. The total time until the unemployment spell ends, which we'll call T, follows an exponential distribution with a rate parameter of lambda plus delta. Consequently, the expected value of T, or the average duration of unemployment, is one divided by the sum of lambda and delta. Now, conditional on the unemployment spell actually ending, the probability that it ends in employment is: lambda divided by the sum of lambda and delta. And the probability of exiting the labor force is: delta divided by the sum of lambda and delta. This illustrates the fundamental probability calculation used in continuous-time competing-risks models. A counting process, which we'll call N of t, for any time t greater than or equal to zero, is defined as a homogeneous Poisson process with a positive rate parameter, lambda, if it meets certain conditions. First, the count at time zero, N of zero, must be zero. Second, it must have independent increments. And third, for any two times, s and t, where s is less than t and both are non-negative, the difference in counts between time t and time s, written as N of t minus N of s, follows a Poisson distribution. The mean parameter for this Poisson distribution is lambda multiplied by the difference between t and s. Specifically, the count at time t, N of t, follows a Poisson distribution with a mean parameter of lambda times t. Furthermore, both the expected value of N at time t, and the variance of N at time t, are equal to lambda times t. Let's denote the time of the first arrival as T sub one. Then, the probability that T sub one is greater than t is equal to the probability that the count at time t, N of t, is zero. This probability is given by the mathematical constant e, raised to the power of negative lambda times t. Therefore, T sub one follows an exponential distribution with a rate parameter of lambda. The property of independent increments means that the times between successive arrivals, known as interarrival times, are independently and identically distributed exponential random variables. Consequently, the time of the r-th arrival, which we'll call T sub r, is the sum of r such interarrival times. If we denote each interarrival time as W sub j, where j goes from one to r, then T sub r is equal to W sub one plus W sub two, and so on, up to W sub r. Each W sub j is independently and identically distributed, following an exponential distribution with rate parameter lambda. This means that T sub r itself follows a Gamma distribution with a shape parameter of r and a rate parameter of lambda. This model effectively unifies the Poisson, exponential, and Gamma families of distributions through a single framework for arrival processes. 13.4 Superposition and thinning of Poisson processes Section 13.4: Superposition and Thinning of Poisson Processes. If we have two independent Poisson processes, N sub one of t and N sub two of t, with their respective rates, lambda sub one and lambda sub two, then their sum, N of t, defined as N sub one of t plus N sub two of t, also forms a Poisson process. The rate for this combined process is the sum of their individual rates: lambda sub one plus lambda sub two. This concept is the process-level equivalent of the statistical principle that independent Poisson random variables can be added together. Conversely, imagine a Poisson process with a rate of lambda. Suppose each arrival in this process is independently categorized as either type one, with a probability of p, or type two, with a probability of one minus p. In this scenario, the counting processes for each specific type are themselves independent Poisson processes. The rate for type one arrivals is p multiplied by lambda, and the rate for type two arrivals is one minus p, all multiplied by lambda. This characteristic, known as the thinning property, proves valuable whenever a single stream of arrivals is randomly split into different destinations, categories, or outcomes. 13.5 Probability integral transform Section 13.5: Probability Integral Transform. Proposition 13.3, the Probability Integral Transform, states the following: If a random variable, X, has a continuous cumulative distribution function, which we'll call F, then a new variable, U, defined as F of X, will follow a uniform distribution between zero and one. Conversely, if U follows a uniform distribution between zero and one, and F inverse represents the generalized inverse of the cumulative distribution function F, then a variable X, defined as F inverse of U, will have the cumulative distribution function F. To illustrate the forward direction of this transform: if we consider any value u between zero and one, and if F is a strictly increasing function, then the probability that F of X is less than or equal to u is equivalent to the probability that X is less than or equal to F inverse of u. Both of these probabilities are simply equal to u. The use of the generalized inverse formulation allows this result to be applied even in cases where F is not strictly increasing. 13.6 Inverse-transform simulation Section 13.6: Inverse-Transform Simulation. The probability integral transform provides a general method for simulation. This recipe involves first generating a random variable U that follows a uniform distribution between zero and one. Then, the desired random variable X is obtained by applying the inverse of the cumulative distribution function F to U, so X equals F inverse of U.
